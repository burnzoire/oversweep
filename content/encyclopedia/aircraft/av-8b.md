## The jump jet grows up

The first Harriers proved that a combat jet could take off and land like a helicopter, but they paid for it. In a short take-off the US Marine Corps' AV-8A carried less than half the bomb load of the smaller A-4 Skyhawk, and over a shorter radius. [[1]](#source-1) In 1973 Hawker Siddeley and McDonnell Douglas set out to double the Harrier's payload and range, starting with a bigger Pegasus engine. Britain left the project for budget reasons in 1975, so McDonnell Douglas carried on alone and rebuilt the design as the AV-8B: a larger composite wing with an extra weapons station on each side, a raised cockpit, a new fuselage and an improved Pegasus. [[1]](#source-1) Britain rejoined in 1981, and the AV-8B first flew in November 1981. The Marines declared it ready for operations with VMA-331 in January 1985. [[1]](#source-1)

## How a Harrier flies

Everything depends on one engine. The Pegasus turbofan exhausts through four nozzles, two each side, that the pilot can rotate. Pointed down, they lift the aircraft straight up; pointed aft, they push it forward like any other jet; set in between, they give a short rolling take-off, which lets the aircraft lift far more weight than it could vertically. [[1]](#source-1) That freedom let the Marines base their own jets on amphibious assault ships and on rough forward sites ashore, close to the troops they supported. [[1]](#source-1)

The price was a demanding aircraft. Take-off and landing are the most dangerous moments of any flight, and Harriers spend more time in them than most. In Marine service the AV-8B's accident rate was about three times that of the Corps' [F/A-18s](record:fa-18c), and some in the military called it a "widow maker". Later analysis blamed much of that record on cuts to senior maintenance staff, pilot error and senior officers who did not understand the aircraft. [[1]](#source-1)

## Night Attack and radar

Upgrades gave it night-attack equipment, as the **AV-8B(NA)**, and then an APG-65 radar in the **Harrier II Plus**. [[1]](#source-1) The Night Attack aircraft fly with the uprated Pegasus F402-RR-408 of about 22,200 lb thrust. [[2]](#source-2) The gun is a 25 mm GAU-12 split between two pods under the fuselage, one holding the gun and the other its ammunition. [[1]](#source-1)

## Into action

The AV-8B's war began on the morning of 17 January 1991, when an OV-10 [forward air controller](term:fac) called for help against Iraqi artillery shelling the Saudi town of Khafji. During the Gulf War 86 Harriers flew 3,380 [sorties](term:sortie) with an availability rate above 90 per cent; five were lost to surface-to-air missiles. General Norman Schwarzkopf later named the AV-8B among seven weapons that played a crucial role in the war, alongside the F-117 and the [AH-64 Apache](record:ah-64d). [[1]](#source-1)

Marine Harriers went on to fly over Kosovo, Afghanistan and Iraq, where the street fighting around Fallujah demanded extreme precision, and over Libya in 2011. [[1]](#source-1) Their worst single day came on 14 September 2012, when a Taliban raid on Camp Bastion in Afghanistan destroyed six AV-8Bs of VMFA-211 on the ground and badly damaged two more, described as the worst loss of US airpower in a single incident since the Vietnam War. [[1]](#source-1) Near the end, in the Red Sea in 2024, a Harrier modified for [air defence](term:air-defense) was reported to have shot down seven Houthi attack drones. [[1]](#source-1)

The Marine Corps retired its Harriers in June 2026, replacing them with the F-35B. Spain and Italy fly their own AV-8Bs from their carriers. [[1]](#source-1)
