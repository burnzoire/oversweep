## A bomber engine in a fighter

The Fw 190's BMW radial had proved unreliable, and the Luftwaffe wanted a version that performed better at high altitude. Kurt Tank's answer, which he saw only as a stopgap until his Ta 152 was ready, was to replace the radial with a liquid-cooled Junkers Jumo 213, a V-12 until then used only in bombers. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) Prototypes began testing in 1942 with a longer nose and a redesigned tail, and small pre-production batches went to units for evaluation in 1943, just as the American Eighth Air Force began large daylight raids. [[2]](#source-2)

## Long-Nose Dora

The first production version was called the D-9, because the last production Fw 190 fighter had been the A-8. German radio used "Dora" for the letter D, so it was the **Dora-9**, and Allied and Luftwaffe pilots alike soon called it the "long-nose" (*Langnasen*) Fw 190. [[2]](#source-2) Pilots liked it. It was more reliable than the radial-engined A-8 and much better in climb, dive and level speed, reaching 709 km/h, and many considered it more than a match for the P-51D Mustang. [[2]](#source-2)

## Too late

The D-9 entered service in October 1944 with III./JG 54. On its first mission with the new fighter, II./JG 26 shot down four Lancaster bombers and a Mosquito for the loss of one Dora. [[2]](#source-2) But by late 1944 Germany's war had changed. Doras flew more often against Allied fighters and in ground attack than against the heavy bombers they had been built to stop. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) On 1 January 1945 Dora units joined Operation Bodenplatte, an attack by nearly 1,000 German aircraft on Allied airfields that cost about 250 fighters; JG 2 lost 40% of its aircraft. [[2]](#source-2) By April 1945 bombing had so starved the Luftwaffe of fuel that when JG 6 received 150 D-9s, only four could fly at a time. [[2]](#source-2)

Only 650 to 700 Fw 190 Ds were built. The design led directly to the Ta 152 high-altitude fighter. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)
