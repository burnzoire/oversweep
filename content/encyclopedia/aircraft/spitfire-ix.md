## From racing seaplanes

Reginald Mitchell designed every British winner of the Schneider Trophy seaplane races after the First World War, and in 1931 his S.6B won the trophy outright for Britain. [[3]](#source-3) His first attempt at a fighter, the Type 224, was a disappointment: an open-cockpit monoplane with gull wings and fixed wheels that lost to a biplane. Mitchell's team started again from what they had learned on the racers, adding a retractable undercarriage, an enclosed cockpit, thin wings and Rolls-Royce's new PV XII engine, soon to be named the **Merlin**. When the Air Ministry asked for eight guns instead of four, the design was ready. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3)

Its famous wing solved two conflicting needs. It had to be thin for low drag, yet thick enough to hold the undercarriage, guns and ammunition, and Mitchell's team chose a near-elliptical shape, the most efficient for producing lift with the least drag. [[1]](#source-1) On 5 March 1936 test pilot "Mutt" Summers took the prototype up for eight minutes from Eastleigh and, on landing, is quoted as saying: "Don't touch anything." [[1]](#source-1) Mitchell never saw it enter production. He died of cancer in June 1937, aged 42. [[3]](#source-3)

## The Battle of Britain

The first Spitfire reached No. 19 Squadron at Duxford on 4 August 1938, and 400 were in service when war broke out. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3) In the Battle of Britain in 1940 the more numerous Hurricane did most of the fighting against the bombers, while Spitfires, with their higher performance, were sent where possible against the German escort fighters; Spitfire [squadrons](term:squadron) lost fewer aircraft and scored more victories for each loss. The legend grew all the same, helped by a "Spitfire Fund" that let the public buy fighters. [[1]](#source-1)

## The Mark IX answers the Fw 190

In 1941 the Focke-Wulf Fw 190 appeared over France and was clearly better than the Spitfire V. The answer was the Merlin 61, with a two-stage supercharger, which test pilot Jeffrey Quill called a "quantum leap". Rather than wait a year for a redesigned airframe, the Air Ministry had existing Spitfire V airframes fitted with the new engine, creating the Mark IX. [[2]](#source-2) Trials in April 1942 found it "outstandingly better than the Spitfire V especially at heights above 20,000 feet": at 38,000 ft it could still fly at 368 mph and manoeuvre well enough to fight. [[2]](#source-2) A stripped Mark IX later caught a high-flying Junkers Ju 86R bomber at over 40,000 ft. [[2]](#source-2)

The **LF Mk IX**, with the Merlin 66 rated for lower altitudes, became the most numerous Mark IX, with more than 4,000 built; it first went into action with the Biggin Hill Wing in March 1943, including the Free French of 341 Squadron. [[2]](#source-2) From early 1944 it carried the Mark II gyro gunsight, which worked out how far to aim ahead of a turning target; it doubled the effectiveness of RAF gunnery. [[2]](#source-2)

## Everywhere, for everyone

Spitfires defended Malta, served with the Soviet [air defence](term:air-defense) forces, and fought the Japanese over Burma and northern Australia. [[1]](#source-1) Americans flew them too: the 4th Fighter Group, formed from the RAF's Eagle Squadrons of American volunteers, kept its Spitfires until March 1943. [[1]](#source-1) Famous Spitfire pilots included "Johnnie" Johnson, with 34 victories, Douglas Bader, "Paddy" Finucane, Canada's George Beurling and South Africa's "Sailor" Malan. [[1]](#source-1) Unarmed photo-reconnaissance Spitfires photographed German radar sites and the V-weapon base at Peenemünde, and one, in a test dive in 1944, reached [Mach](term:mach-number) 0.92, the fastest ever recorded for a piston-engined aircraft. [[1]](#source-1)

When the last one rolled out in February 1948, 20,351 Spitfires had been built. It was the only British fighter built continuously before, during and after the war. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3)
