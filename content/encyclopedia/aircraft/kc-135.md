## The first jet tanker

In 1950 the US Air Force's tanker was the KC-97 Stratofreighter, a piston-engined aircraft burning gasoline, with a Boeing flying boom and extra tanks of jet fuel for its receivers. It cruised at about 230 mph, so the new jets had to slow right down to take its boom. [[1]](#source-1) Its replacement grew from the same Boeing 367-80 "Dash-80" demonstrator as the 707 airliner, though the KC-135 came first, is shorter and narrower, and is structurally quite different. [[1]](#source-1) It first flew in August 1956 and reached Castle Air Force Base, California, in June 1957; the last of 803 was delivered in 1965. [[1]](#source-1)

## Boom first, drogue when needed

The boom operator lies prone in the tail, looking out through a window at the bottom of the fuselage, and flies the boom, much as on the KC-97. [[1]](#source-1) Probe-equipped Navy and Marine Corps aircraft need a drogue instead: a short hose and hard drogue can be fitted to the boom on the ground. That adapter has no hose response, so it is far less forgiving of the receiver's flying, and while it is fitted the boom cannot refuel receptacle aircraft. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The Multi-point Refueling System adds a Mk 32B hose pod under each wing, keeping the boom and refuelling two probe receivers at once. [[1]](#source-1)

## Sixty years on the job

The original J57 turbojets used water injection for takeoff thrust. Re-engining with CFM56 turbofans, as the F108, created the KC-135R and T, which can offload up to 50% more fuel on a long sortie, burn 25% less, and are far quieter. [[1]](#source-1) Over Vietnam, KC-135 crews refuelled Air Force, Navy and Marine aircraft, and sometimes kept damaged aircraft with holed tanks fed with fuel all the way to a landing or a ditching. [[1]](#source-1) It is one of the few military aircraft to pass 60 years with its original operator, and it is being partly replaced by the [KC-46](record:kc-46). [[1]](#source-1) France flew 12 new-built C-135FR tankers with a drogue adapter from 1964 until A330 MRTTs replaced them, and they now serve a private refuelling company. [[1]](#source-1)
