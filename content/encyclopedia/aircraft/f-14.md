## Guarding the fleet

From the late 1950s the US Navy had a nightmare: Soviet jet bombers and submarines firing long-range anti-ship missiles at its carrier battle groups from beyond the reach of their fighters. It wanted a fleet air defence fighter with a bigger radar and longer-range missiles than the F-4 Phantom, able to shoot down both the bombers and their missiles far out from the carrier. [[1]](#source-1)

The first answer was forced on the Navy. Defense Secretary Robert McNamara wanted the Navy and Air Force to share one aircraft, and the result, the F-111B, was heavy and underpowered. Vice Admiral Thomas Connolly flew an F-111, found it struggled to go supersonic and was poor at carrier landings, and told Congress so, against the Navy's official line. In May 1968 Congress stopped funding the F-111B. [[1]](#source-1) The new fighter's nickname, "Tom's Cat", honoured Connolly, and as the **Tomcat** it joined Grumman's long line of feline fighters, from the Wildcat and Hellcat of the Second World War to the Panther and Cougar jets. [[1]](#source-1)

## Swing wings and Phoenix missiles

Grumman won the contract in January 1969, and to save time and avoid cancellation the Navy skipped prototypes and went straight to full-scale development. The F-14 first flew on 21 December 1970, just 22 months later. [[1]](#source-1) It was the largest and heaviest US fighter ever to fly from a carrier, because it had to carry the huge AWG-9 radar and the AIM-54 Phoenix missile. [[1]](#source-1)

Its variable-sweep wings are the answer to the carrier fighter's oldest problem. Spread to 64 ft, they let the Tomcat approach the carrier at about 125 kt; swept back to 38 ft, they let it dash. [[2]](#source-2) The pilot sits in front, and the radar intercept officer (RIO) behind runs the radar and the missiles. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) In tests in 1973 a Phoenix hit a target 110 nautical miles away, and six were fired within 38 seconds at six targets, scoring four direct hits. [[1]](#source-1) In practice no Tomcat ever deployed with six Phoenixes: there was no threat that called for it, and the load was too heavy to land back aboard the carrier if the missiles were not fired. A typical Cold War load was two Phoenixes, three Sparrows and two Sidewinders, plus the internal M61 gun. [[1]](#source-1)

The F-14A's TF30 engines were its weak point. From the late 1980s the F-14B and F-14D replaced them with the much more powerful General Electric F110. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)

## Over the Gulf of Sidra

Tomcats replaced Phantoms in the fleet from September 1974 and flew cover for the evacuation of Saigon. [[1]](#source-1) Their first kills came on 19 August 1981, when two F-14s of VF-41 "Black Aces" were fired on by two Libyan Su-22s over the Gulf of Sidra, evaded the missile and shot both down with Sidewinders. On 4 January 1989, in the same waters, two F-14s of VF-32 shot down two Libyan MiG-23s. [[1]](#source-1)

In the 1990s the Tomcat learned to bomb. With the LANTIRN pod, which the RIO used to guide laser-guided bombs, the "Bombcat" flew precision strikes until the end of its career. [[1]](#source-1) The 1986 film *Top Gun* made it famous far beyond the Navy; the film so boosted interest in naval aviation that the Navy set up recruiting desks outside some cinemas. [[1]](#source-1)

The Tomcat's official farewell came on 22 September 2006 at NAS Oceana, flown in a backup aircraft after the primary jet developed a fault. The Super Hornet took over its missions. [[1]](#source-1) The retired Tomcats were later shredded so that their parts could not reach the only other country that flew them. [[1]](#source-1)

## Iran's Tomcats

That country was Iran. The Shah wanted a fighter that could catch Soviet MiG-25 reconnaissance flights, and in 1974 Iran ordered 80 Tomcats and 714 Phoenix missiles; the first arrived in January 1976. [[1]](#source-1) After the 1979 revolution Iran's air force flew them through the Iran–Iraq War, and Iran claimed its F-14s shot down at least 160 Iraqi aircraft, 55 of them confirmed, for the loss of 16 Tomcats. [[1]](#source-1) Cut off from American spares, Iran kept a few flying for decades; several of the survivors were destroyed on the ground in Israeli air strikes in 2025 and 2026. [[1]](#source-1)
