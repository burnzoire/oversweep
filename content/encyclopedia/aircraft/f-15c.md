## "Not a pound for air-to-ground"

In 1967 the Soviet Union showed off a new fighter at Domodedovo airfield near Moscow. The MiG-25 had a huge wing and was built for speeds above [Mach](term:mach-number) 2.8, and to Western observers it looked as if the United States was about to be outclassed: it seemed faster, higher-flying and longer-legged than any American fighter, including the one still on the drawing board. The US Air Force's answer was to sharpen its F-X project into a pure [air superiority](term:air-superiority) fighter. [[1]](#source-1) Its design team's slogan said it all: "Not a pound for air-to-ground." [[2]](#source-2)

McDonnell Douglas won the contract in 1969. The Eagle first flew in July 1972 and entered service in 1976, replacing the [F-4 Phantom II](record:f-4). [[1]](#source-1) It was built to fly higher and faster than the [Phantom](record:f-4), climb and accelerate far better, and give its pilot an all-round view and a radar that could look down and shoot down at low-flying targets. [[2]](#source-2)

## Power to spare

The Eagle's secret is a big wing and a lot of thrust. Low wing loading lets it turn hard at up to 9 g without bleeding airspeed, it can climb to 30,000 ft in about a minute, and at some speeds its two F100 engines produce more thrust than the aircraft's weight and drag, so it can accelerate going straight up. [[1]](#source-1)

The Air Force proved it in 1975. An unpainted F-15A stripped of most of its avionics, the "Streak Eagle", broke eight time-to-climb world records from Grand Forks, North Dakota, between 16 January and 1 February. It now stands in the National Museum of the US Air Force. [[1]](#source-1) All that thrust came at a price in the early years: the F100-PW-100 engines were unreliable, not helped by pilots slamming the throttles about far more than in earlier fighters, until the improved F100-PW-220 arrived in 1986. [[1]](#source-1)

The airframe is famously tough. In 1983 an Israeli F-15D collided with an A-4 Skyhawk over the Negev and lost most of its right wing. Using full [afterburner](term:afterburner), and landing at twice the normal speed, pilot Zivi Nedivi brought it home to Ramon Airbase. Wind-tunnel tests later showed that controlled flight on one wing was possible only within a very narrow band of speed and [angle of attack](term:aoa). [[1]](#source-1)

## Weapons and sensors

The F-15C carries up to eight air-to-air missiles: Sidewinders on the wing pylons and Sparrows or AMRAAMs on the fuselage stations, with an internal M61 gun and 940 rounds. [[2]](#source-2) Everything is laid out so that one pilot can fight alone. [[1]](#source-1) The original APG-63 radar was later replaced on some aircraft by active electronically scanned array (AESA) versions. [[1]](#source-1)

## 104 to nothing

Israel flew the Eagle into combat first, and its pilot Moshe Melnik scored the first F-15 kill in 1979. In the 1982 Lebanon War Israeli F-15s were credited with 41 Syrian aircraft. [[1]](#source-1)

In the 1991 Gulf War, F-15s accounted for 36 of the US Air Force's 39 air-to-air victories over Iraq. The C and D fighters held the air while the [F-15E](record:f-15e) Strike Eagles hunted Scud launchers at night. [[1]](#source-1) Across all its users the F-15 has a combined record of 104 kills, and the [air superiority](term:air-superiority) versions have never been lost to enemy action in air combat. More than half of those kills were scored by Israeli pilots. [[1]](#source-1)

One of the strangest Eagle missions was flown in space. From 1984 to 1986 two F-15As served as the first stage of the ASM-135 anti-satellite missile. On the third test, Major Wilbert "Doug" Pearson's missile destroyed a working solar observatory satellite in orbit, making him the only pilot ever to destroy a satellite. The programme ended in 1988. [[1]](#source-1)

The F-22 began replacing the F-15C in the 2000s. Fewer F-22s were bought than planned, so the Eagle soldiered on, mostly with the Air National Guard, and the Air Force added the new F-15EX alongside it. The last active-duty F-15C flight was flown from Kadena in Japan on 24 January 2025. [[1]](#source-1) The design's untapped strike potential became the two-seat **F-15E Strike Eagle**. [[2]](#source-2)
