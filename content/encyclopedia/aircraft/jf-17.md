## A fighter nobody could cut off

Pakistan learned the hard way what it means to depend on someone else's fighters. In 1989 US sanctions ended Project Sabre II, a design study with Grumman and China; that year Grumman also left a Chinese upgrade of the F-7, the Super 7, after sanctions followed the Tiananmen Square protests. [[1]](#source-1) Pakistan's Air Force wanted an affordable, modern, lightweight multirole fighter that no sanctions could take away from it, to replace a large fleet of ageing A-5C bombers, F-7 interceptors and Mirage III and 5 aircraft. The cost was split equally between Pakistan and China. [[1]](#source-1)

The project, launched in 1991 as the Fighter China-1 (FC-1), stalled when Pakistan, watching India buy the Su-30MKI, asked for higher performance; a 1998 agreement restarted it. [[2]](#source-2) Sanctions after Pakistan's 1998 nuclear tests slowed it again by blocking Western avionics, so in 2001 the air force decided to design the airframe first and fit the avionics later. [[1]](#source-1) Its chief designer, Yang Wei, would go on to design China's J-20 stealth fighter. [[1]](#source-1)

## Thunder

The first prototype was rolled out on 31 May 2003. Its first flight slipped from June because of the SARS outbreak, and around this time the name Super 7 gave way to **JF-17**, for "Joint Fighter". [[1]](#source-1) Testing brought changes: the air intakes were widened because the RD-93 engine smoked too much, the wing root extensions were altered to fix control problems, and the fin was enlarged to hold electronic warfare equipment in its tip. [[1]](#source-1) The RD-93 belongs to the same Russian engine family as the MiG-29's. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)

Its gun is a twin-barrel GSh-23, and its missiles are mostly Chinese: the PL-5 short-range missile and the SD-10 for fights beyond visual range, with air-to-ground and anti-ship weapons too. [[1]](#source-1) Serial production of the **Block 3**, with an AESA radar and the RD-93MA engine, began in December 2020. [[1]](#source-1)

## In service

The first two small-batch JF-17s reached Pakistan in March 2007 and flew in the Pakistan Day parade over Islamabad that month. The first Pakistani-built aircraft followed in November 2009, and No. 26 Squadron, the "Black Spiders", became the first JF-17 squadron in February 2010. [[1]](#source-1) It flew its first combat sorties against militants in South Waziristan and later struck Taliban hideouts during Operation Zarb-e-Azb from 2014 to 2016. [[1]](#source-1)

In February 2019 two JF-17s took part in Pakistan's retaliatory strikes on India, and in the May 2025 conflict with India the air force sent JF-17s into combat in both air-to-air and air-to-ground roles, publicly showing a Block 3 armed with long-range PL-15E missiles for the first time. Many of the claims made on both sides in that conflict are disputed. [[1]](#source-1) More than 200 JF-17s have been built for Pakistan and for export customers including Myanmar, Nigeria and Azerbaijan. [[1]](#source-1)
