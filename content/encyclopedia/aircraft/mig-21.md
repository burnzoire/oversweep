## The balalaika

Seen from below, the MiG-21's triangular wing and slim fuselage look like a stringed instrument, and Soviet pilots nicknamed it the *Balalaika*. Poles called it *Ołówek*, "pencil", and the Vietnamese *Én Bạc*, "silver swallow". About 60 countries on four continents have flown it, and 11,496 were built: more than any other [supersonic](term:supersonic) jet in history. [[1]](#source-1)

## From swept wing to delta

The MiG-21 grew out of the [MiG-15](record:mig-15), MiG-17 and [supersonic](term:supersonic) [MiG-19](record:mig-19). Mikoyan's first prototypes of the early 1950s had swept wings; the Ye-4 of 1955 was the first with the tailed delta wing of the production aircraft, and it appeared in public at the Tushino air show in July 1956. [[1]](#source-1) Western intelligence had so little to go on that *Jane's All the World's Aircraft* for 1960–61 listed the "Fishbed" as a Sukhoi design and illustrated it with a picture of a different fighter. [[1]](#source-1)

It was the first successful Soviet aircraft to combine fighter and interceptor in one airframe: a lightweight that reached [Mach](term:mach-number) 2 on a modest engine, comparable to the American F-104 and [F-5](record:f-5) and the French Mirage III. Its controls, engine and avionics were simple, its tail helped keep it controllable at the edges of the envelope, and it was cheap to build and run; a MiG-21MF cost less than a BMP-1 infantry fighting vehicle. That made it ideal for air forces with small budgets and short training programmes. [[1]](#source-1)

Simplicity had costs. Fuel tanks sat ahead of the centre of gravity, so as fuel burned the aircraft became unstable, and endurance without external tanks was only about 45 minutes. The delta wing climbed superbly but bled speed quickly in a turning fight. The final **MiG-21bis** answered with brute force: its R-25 engine had an emergency rating, usable for two minutes, that gave it a thrust-to-weight ratio slightly better than 1:1 and a climb rate close to an early [F-16](record:f-16)'s. The Soviets called it the "diamond regime", after the shock diamonds glowing in its 10–12 m exhaust flame. [[1]](#source-1)

## Silver swallows over Vietnam

The first MiG-21s reached North Vietnam by ship in April 1966 and went to the 921st Fighter Regiment. On 7 July 1966 a rocket-armed MiG-21 shot down an F-105D near Phuc Yen, the type's first victory over a piloted US aircraft in the war. [[1]](#source-1) Built for short, ground-controlled interceptions, it became a dangerous adversary in experienced hands, making high-speed hit-and-run attacks on F-105 strike formations that downed some and forced others to jettison their bombs. Thirteen North Vietnamese pilots became aces in MiG-21s; the most successful, Nguyễn Văn Cốc, scored nine kills. The ace Nguyễn Nhật Chiêu said: "In general combat conditions, I was always confident of a kill over an [F-4 Phantom](record:f-4) when flying a MiG-21." [[1]](#source-1) US Air Force [F-4s](record:f-4) and B-52s claimed 68 MiG-21s between April 1965 and January 1973. [[2]](#source-2)

## Deserts and mountains

Egyptian, Syrian and Iraqi MiG-21s fought Israel from 1964. In the 1967 Six-Day War Egypt lost about 100 of its roughly 110 MiG-21s, almost all of them on the ground. In 1970 Soviet pilots flew MiG-21MFs over Egypt during the War of Attrition and shot down several Israeli aircraft. Syrian MiG-21 pilots independently discovered the Cobra manoeuvre, which they called the "zero speed manoeuvre". [[1]](#source-1)

India chose the MiG-21 in 1961 and inducted more than 1,200 of them. In the 1971 war Indian MiG-21FLs fought the subcontinent's first [supersonic](term:supersonic) dogfight and claimed four Pakistani F-104As. It also earned a darker nickname, the "flying coffin", after more than 170 Indian pilots were killed in MiG-21 accidents from 1970 on. India retired its last MiG-21 Bisons in August 2025, with a farewell ceremony at Chandigarh on 26 September. [[1]](#source-1)

## Copied in China

The Soviets licensed the MiG-21F and its engine to China in 1961, and the first Shenyang-built J-7 flew on 17 January 1966. The J-7 became China's most-produced fighter, and by 1989 the factories were turning out as many as 14 a month, mostly for export; Pakistan took F-7s from 1988. [[2]](#source-2) Romanian, Israeli and Russian companies still offer upgrades to keep MiG-21s flying, seven decades after the first flight. [[1]](#source-1)
