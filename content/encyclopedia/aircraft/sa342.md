## A fan in the tail

Look at the back of a Gazelle and something is missing: there is no tail rotor. In its place, built into the fin, is a ring of 13 small blades inside a shroud, the **fenestron**. The Gazelle was the first helicopter to have one. The shrouded fan costs a little more power when hovering, but it is far harder to damage, much safer for anyone walking near the helicopter, and needs less power at cruising speed. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The first prototype flew on 7 April 1967 with an ordinary tail rotor borrowed from the Alouette II; the second prototype got the fenestron in early 1968. [[1]](#source-1)

The rotor blades were new as well: made mostly of fibreglass, they were designed for an extremely long life, and composite blades later became common across the industry. Getting the main rotor right took a long time. The first design was a [rigid rotor](term:rigid-rotor) based on the Bo 105's, but it gave control problems at high speed and production aircraft used an articulated rotor instead. [[1]](#source-1) The result was fast. On 13 May 1967 a Gazelle set two world speed records on a closed course, 307 km/h over 3 km and 292 km/h over 100 km, and the fenestron has been credited with making it the fastest helicopter in its class. [[1]](#source-1)

## Easy to fly, easy to keep

Pilots found the Gazelle docile. It is reported to fly comfortably at up to 100 knots even with its main hydraulic system out, and unusually it has no throttle and no trim system. It was the first helicopter adapted for a single pilot to fly on instruments, with a Honeywell duplex autopilot; it was chosen for that work because it was fast, stable and known to be easy to fly. [[1]](#source-1) Maintenance was cut to the bone: bearings were life-rated and needed no regular lubrication, and many components were designed to run 700 hours, some 1,200, before replacement. [[1]](#source-1)

## An Anglo-French helicopter

Britain wanted it from the start. In February 1967 France and the UK signed a deal under which Westland would build Gazelles and Pumas for the British forces while Sud Aviation shared in the Lynx. [[1]](#source-1) The Gazelle went on to serve all three British services. The British Army's first operational unit, No. 660 Squadron Army Air Corps in Germany, received it on 6 July 1974 to replace the Sioux, and used it for [reconnaissance](term:reconnaissance), directing artillery fire, casualty evacuation and anti-tank work. [[1]](#source-1) In the Falklands War in 1982 Gazelles were rushed into action with rocket pods, armour and flotation gear: two Royal Marines Gazelles were shot down on the first day of the San Carlos landings, and on 6 June an Army Gazelle was mistaken for a low-flying Argentine [C-130](record:c-130j) and shot down by the destroyer HMS *Cardiff*. [[1]](#source-1) The British Army's Cold War Gazelles flew more than 660,000 hours. The last ones were retired on 31 October 2023 after almost 50 years, with a farewell flypast from Northern Ireland a week before. [[1]](#source-1)

## Tank hunter

In the French Army the Gazelle was above all a tank hunter. The SA 342M carries four HOT wire-guided missiles and a stabilised sight on the cabin roof; other French versions carry a 20 mm cannon or Mistral missiles to shoot down enemy helicopters, and the Gazelle Viviane adds a thermal imager for night work. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The Tiger has replaced it as France's front-line attack helicopter. [[1]](#source-1)

The Gazelle's HOT missiles made their name in the 1982 Lebanon War. Syrian Gazelles struck the advancing Israeli army repeatedly, using Western-style "pop-up" tactics; panic spread among Israeli tank crews after the first raid on 8 June, and the missiles' range was a key reason. Syria later claimed 30 tanks destroyed for the loss of five helicopters, while Israel admitted seven tanks lost and claimed 12 Gazelles shot down. One analyst judged the raids psychologically effective but low on actual kills. [[1]](#source-1)

## Everywhere at once

The Gazelle has fought on both sides of more wars than most aircraft. Iraq flew about 100 against Iran in the 1980s; in 1991 Kuwaiti Gazelles that had escaped to Saudi Arabia flew back to attack Iraqi tanks, and more than 50 French Gazelles strafed Iraqi armour in continuous high-speed waves. [[1]](#source-1) On 27 June 1991 a Yugoslav Air Force Gazelle was brought down by a shoulder-launched missile over Ljubljana, the first aircraft lost in the break-up of Yugoslavia. [[1]](#source-1) French Gazelles have flown from Chad to Afghanistan, fired on Somali pirates in 2008, struck Libyan forces from the assault ship *Tonnerre* in 2011, and attacked Gbagbo's compound in Côte d'Ivoire the same year. [[1]](#source-1) As recently as 2021, three Gazelles supported Mozambican forces and helped evacuate civilians during the Battle of Palma. [[1]](#source-1)
