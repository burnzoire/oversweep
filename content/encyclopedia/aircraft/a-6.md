## A bomber for any weather

In Korea, the Navy's propeller-driven A-1 Skyraiders could only support troops on the ground when the weather was fair. The Navy wanted a carrier jet that could find and hit a target at night and through cloud, a "close air support attack bomber capable of hitting the enemy at any time", and it issued the requirement in February 1957. Eleven designs from eight companies competed, and on 2 January 1958 Grumman's won. [[1]](#source-1)

The prototype first flew on 19 April 1960, and the Intruder joined the fleet in February 1963, called by aviation historians "the first genuinely all-weather attack bomber in history". [[1]](#source-1) It was the first Navy aircraft whose airframe and weapon system were designed as one. [[1]](#source-1)

## Two seats, side by side

The Intruder is built around its crew. The pilot sits on the left and the bombardier/navigator (BN) on the right and slightly lower, so the pilot can see across the cockpit. The BN runs the radar and the attack system; the pilot flies from a screen called the Vertical Display Indicator, which drew a synthetic picture of the world ahead with steering cues from the BN, so the crew could navigate and attack head-down at night and in cloud. [[1]](#source-1)

There is no afterburner and no gun. The two J52 turbojets give a high [subsonic](term:) speed, and the wing was chosen for efficiency and manoeuvrability with a heavy bomb load rather than for speed. A very similar wing later went onto pivots on Grumman's [F-14 Tomcat](record:f-14). [[1]](#source-1) The wingtips split open above and below to make the speed brakes, and the tailpipes are angled seven degrees down, a leftover of the [STOL](term:) performance the Marines first asked for. [[1]](#source-1) Its large blunt nose and slender tail earned it the nicknames "Double Ugly" and "Iron Tadpole". [[1]](#source-1)

The A-6E, first deployed in December 1971, replaced the A-6A's separate search and tracking radars with one multimode radar and new computers. [[1]](#source-1) From 1979 its TRAM turret under the nose added a FLIR and a laser designator, so the BN could find a target on infrared, mark it and drop the jet's own [laser-guided bombs](term:lgw) with no help from another aircraft. The crew could also attack without turning on the radar, which might warn the target, and drop on a target they could not see by working from an [offset aimpoint](term:) nearby. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The Manual's [Precision Targeting](module:precision-targeting) module covers this kind of attack.

## Vietnam to the Gulf

The Intruder went to war in Vietnam, flying from carriers and from Marine bases at Chu Lai and Da Nang. Its range, its 18,000 lb load and its ability to fly in any weather made it invaluable, but the low-level attacks it flew were costly: of 84 Intruders lost in eight years, 56 fell to guns and anti-aircraft artillery, 10 to surface-to-air missiles and 2 to MiGs. [[1]](#source-1)

In April 1986 Intruders from USS *America* and *Coral Sea* struck Libya alongside Air Force F-111s. The strike force flew low in complete darkness through alerted air defences, evading over 100 guided missiles, and put laser-guided and other bombs on target. [[2]](#source-2) Two years later, in Operation Praying Mantis, VA-95 Intruders sank the Iranian frigate *Sahand* and badly damaged the *Sabalan*. [[1]](#source-1)

In the 1991 Gulf War Navy and Marine Intruders flew more than 4,700 combat [sorties](term:sortie): [close air support](term:cas), [defence suppression](term:sead), attacks on Iraqi ships and strategic strikes. They were the Navy's main jet for laser-guided bombs, and three were shot down. [[1]](#source-1)

## The tanker in every air wing

A few Intruders in each squadron flew as KA-6D tankers. Their attack system was removed and a refuelling system fitted, sometimes with a D-704 [buddy store](term:) on the centre pylon. Because a KA-6D could keep up with the strike aircraft it was refuelling, it could tank them during a mission rather than only around the carrier. Crews trained on both versions, and the tankers were always in short supply, often handed from a carrier coming home to one deploying. [[1]](#source-1) The Manual's [Air-to-Air Refuelling](module:aar-procedures) module covers receiving fuel from a hose tanker like this one.

## Retirement

The Navy meant to replace the Intruder with the stealthy A-12 Avenger II, and when that was cancelled in 1991 there was no successor. To cut the number of aircraft types in its air wings, it retired the A-6 anyway: the Marines in April 1993 and the Navy on 28 February 1997. [[1]](#source-1) The last Intruder squadron launched from a carrier deck, USS *Enterprise*, on 19 December 1996. [[2]](#source-2) The [LANTIRN](record:lantirn)-equipped [F-14](record:f-14) and then the F/A-18E/F Super Hornet took over its strike work, and the [S-3 Viking](record:s-3b) its tanking. [[1]](#source-1) Many in naval aviation questioned the swap: neither successor matched the Intruder's range and load. [[1]](#source-1)
