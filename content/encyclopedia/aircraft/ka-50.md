## One pilot, two rotors

NATO armies judged a single-seat attack helicopter undesirable. The Ka-50 has one seat. In testing in 1985 and 1986, Soviet evaluators found that the workload was similar to a fighter-bomber pilot's and that one person could fly, navigate and fight. [[1]](#source-1) Its designers aimed for a helicopter that was small, fast and agile, and that could operate for two weeks away from maintenance facilities. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)

Like other Kamov designs, it has two three-bladed rotors stacked one above the other and turning in opposite directions, with no tail rotor. A tail rotor can absorb up to 30% of a helicopter's engine power just countering torque, so the coaxial layout leaves more for lift, and the helicopter can make flat turns at any speed regardless of the wind. It is fully aerobatic: it can loop, roll and fly "the funnel", circling a target at changing heights and speeds while keeping its weapons pointed at it. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) In testing, one Ka-50 lost its tail and still flew back to base, because the tail boom carries no rotor. [[1]](#source-1)

## Painted windows and a werewolf

Western intelligence first heard of the helicopter in 1984 and saw a photograph only in 1989. The first two prototypes had false windows painted on them, which misled early Western reports so thoroughly that some analysts concluded it was a fighter built to hunt NATO helicopters. [[1]](#source-1) At the 1992 Farnborough air show the second production aircraft appeared with a werewolf painted on its rudder, earning it the nickname "Werewolf". The fifth prototype, painted black, then starred in the Russian film *Black Shark*, and that name stuck. NATO calls it the **Hokum-A**. [[1]](#source-1)

## Built to survive

The pilot sits in an armoured cockpit of layered steel and aluminium plates rated against 12.7 mm armour-piercing bullets and 23 mm fragments, and the rotor blades can take several hits from ground fire. [[1]](#source-1) The Ka-50 was the world's first operational helicopter with an ejection seat that works at any height and speed. Before the K-37-800 rocket seat fires, explosive charges blow the rotor blades away and the canopy is jettisoned. [[1]](#source-1)

Its 30 mm cannon, the same weapon as on the BMP-2 infantry fighting vehicle, is fixed almost rigidly to the side of the fuselage near the centre of gravity for accuracy, and the fire-control computer turns the whole helicopter to keep the gun on target. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) Its main anti-tank weapon is the Vikhr [laser-guided](term:lgw) missile, with a range of about 8 km. [[1]](#source-1)

## Too few to replace the Hind

The Ka-50 was cleared for service with the Russian army on 28 August 1995, but the collapse of the Soviet Union cut defence spending so sharply that only about a dozen were delivered, instead of the several hundred planned to replace the [Mi-24](record:mi-24p). [[1]](#source-1) Two production Ka-50s went to Chechnya in December 2000 and first used their weapons in combat on 6 January 2001; in one strike that February a pair destroyed a fortified insurgent camp with Vikhr missiles from 3 km away. [[1]](#source-1) The Mi-28 became the army's main gunship, and the Ka-50 was assigned to support special forces. Its lasting legacy is the two-seat **Ka-52 "Alligator"**, built in far larger numbers and used heavily, with dozens lost, in the Russian invasion of Ukraine. [[1]](#source-1)
