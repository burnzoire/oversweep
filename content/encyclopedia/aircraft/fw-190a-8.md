## Kurt Tank's cavalry horse

In 1937 the German air ministry wanted a second fighter alongside the [Bf 109](record:bf-109k-4), in case foreign designs overtook it. Kurt Tank of Focke-Wulf offered a design built around an air-cooled radial engine. Most Europeans thought a radial too bulky for a small, fast fighter, but Tank had seen the US Navy make radials work, and his choice had a practical advantage: it would not compete with the [Bf 109](record:bf-109k-4) and Bf 110 for scarce Daimler-Benz engines. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3) Tank, who had served in the cavalry and infantry in the First World War, compared the [Bf 109](record:bf-109k-4) and the [Spitfire](record:spitfire-ix) to racehorses that could outrun anything with enough pampering but faltered "the moment the going became tough". He wanted a fighter that could fly from rough front-line airfields, be flown and maintained by men with little training, and take battle damage and still get home: "not a racehorse, but a *Dienstpferd*, a cavalry horse." [[1]](#source-1)

It was full of new ideas. A wide-track landing gear retracted inwards, built to take twice the usual landing loads, so the Fw 190 had far fewer ground accidents than the [Bf 109](record:bf-109k-4). Rigid push-rods instead of cables made the controls light and crisp, and the landing gear, flaps and guns were worked electrically, making it the only fighter of the war with electric landing gear and flaps. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3) A single lever, the *Kommandogerät*, automatically set the engine's revolutions, mixture, timing and supercharger. [[2]](#source-2) Hans Sander flew the first prototype on 1 June 1939; it handled beautifully, but the tight cowling cooked the engine and the pilot, and overheating nearly cancelled the programme. [[3]](#source-3)

## The shock over the Channel

The Fw 190 reached JG 26 in France in August 1941. RAF pilots reported a new radial-engined fighter, and at first intelligence officers assumed they had met captured French Curtiss Hawks. [[2]](#source-2) It outclassed the [Spitfire](record:spitfire-ix) V in everything except turning circle: it rolled faster, hit harder and was faster at low level. Until the [Spitfire](record:spitfire-ix) IX arrived in late 1942, the Allies had nothing to match it. [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3) Plans were drawn up for a commando raid to steal one, but in June 1942 Oberleutnant Armin Faber of JG 2, lost and disoriented, landed his Fw 190 at a British airfield by mistake and handed the RAF an intact example. [[2]](#source-2) German crews called it the **Würger**, the shrike or "butcher bird". [[3]](#source-3)

## Storming the bombers

When American heavy bombers began raiding Germany in daylight, their massed .50 calibre guns made them very hard to bring down. The Fw 190, rugged and a steady gun platform, became the Luftwaffe's bomber destroyer. [[1]](#source-1) The **A-8**, in production from February 1944 and built in greater numbers than any other Fw 190 fighter, carried two machine guns and four 20 mm cannon, and armoured *Sturmböcke* ("battering rams") versions swapped two cannon for heavy 30 mm guns and added armour around the cockpit. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3)

The *Sturmgruppen* attacked from directly behind the bombers, often closing to within 100 yards. When the tactic worked the results were devastating, but the extra weight made the A-8 sluggish, so Bf 109s had to fly escort to keep the American P-47s and P-51s away. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3) On the Eastern Front, its reliable air-cooled engine and wide landing gear suited rough airfields and harsh winters, and ground-attack versions served there until the end of the war. [[3]](#source-3)
