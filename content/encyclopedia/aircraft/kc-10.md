## Born of an airlift

In the 1973 Yom Kippur War the US airlifted weapons to Israel in Operation Nickel Grass. Denied landing rights in Europe, C-5 Galaxy transports flew direct from the United States carrying a fraction of their payload, which showed how short the Air Force was of refuelling capacity. [[1]](#source-1) The Advanced Tanker Cargo Aircraft programme compared the Boeing 747, the C-5 and the DC-10, and in December 1977 chose the DC-10, mainly because it could use shorter runways. An order for 12 grew to 60. [[1]](#source-1)

## Boom and hose on one airframe

The KC-10 kept 88% commonality with the DC-10-30CF airliner, losing most windows and the lower cargo doors and gaining tanks in the baggage holds that nearly doubled the [KC-135](record:kc-135)'s fuel, to 356,000 lb. [[1]](#source-1) Its boom has a fly-by-wire control surface at the end instead of the earlier V-tail, and the boom operator sits upright at a wide rear window rather than lying prone. [[1]](#source-1) A hose and drogue on the right of the rear fuselage meant one mission could refuel Air Force, Navy, Marine Corps and most allied aircraft, and the last 20 added wing pods. It can refuel while carrying cargo. [[1]](#source-1)

## From Libya to retirement

KC-10s supported the 1986 raid on Libya, the Gulf War, the Kosovo air campaign and the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. [[1]](#source-1) The Royal Netherlands Air Force flew two KDC-10s converted from used DC-10s from 1995 to 2021, replacing them with A330 MRTTs; both went to a US contractor, Omega Aerial Refueling Services. [[1]](#source-1) The US Air Force retired its last KC-10 in 2024. [[1]](#source-1)
