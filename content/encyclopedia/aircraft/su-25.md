## A flying tank for the army

In early 1968 the Soviet Ministry of Defence decided it needed a *shturmovik*, a specialised armoured assault aircraft, to support troops on the battlefield. The idea came from studying ground-attack aviation from the 1940s to the 1960s. The fighter-bombers of the day, the Su-7, Su-17, [MiG-21](record:mig-21) and MiG-23, were the wrong tools: they had no armour to protect the pilot from ground fire, and they flew too fast for the pilot to keep a target in sight. Pavel Sukhoi's bureau worked up its T-8 design, beat Ilyushin's Il-102 in the competition, and flew the first prototype on 22 February 1975. Series production began in 1978 at Tbilisi in Soviet Georgia. [[1]](#source-1)

The result was the Su-25 *Grach*, Russian for "rook"; NATO called it the **Frogfoot**. [[1]](#source-1) It is a [subsonic](term:subsonic), twin-engined attack jet with a high, moderately swept wing, built to take punishment; US analysts list the [A-10 Thunderbolt II](record:a-10) among comparable aircraft. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)

## The titanium bathtub

The pilot sits low in a bathtub of welded titanium sheets, with a 6 mm steel plate behind the head. The armour makes the cockpit cramped and the view poor; a periscope on top of the canopy helps the pilot see behind. [[1]](#source-1) A twin-barrel 30 mm cannon sits under the cockpit, and ten wing hardpoints carry about 4,000 kg of bombs and rockets, with small outboard pylons for self-defence missiles. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The wingtip pods split open as air brakes. [[1]](#source-1)

Just as important, the Su-25 was built to keep fighting from primitive bases. Its servicing equipment packs into containers that the aircraft can carry itself, so a [squadron](term:squadron) can move forward and look after its jets without a large ground support train. [[2]](#source-2)

## Afghanistan

Combat came quickly. On 19 July 1981 the 200th Independent Attack Squadron deployed to Shindand in western Afghanistan, the first Su-25 unit sent there, to strike rebel positions in the mountains. Each aircraft flew an average of 360 [sorties](term:sortie) a year, more than any other combat aircraft in the war, and by 1989 nearly 50 Su-25s based in Afghanistan had flown 60,000 [sorties](term:sortie). Between 21 and 23 were lost in combat. [[1]](#source-1) The lessons learned there shaped the next generation of the aircraft. [[2]](#source-2)

## The tank hunter

The **Su-25T** turned the two-seat Su-25UB trainer airframe into a single-seat anti-tank aircraft, with the rear cockpit replaced by fuel and avionics. [[2]](#source-2) Its enlarged nose houses the Shkval TV targeting system with a laser rangefinder and designator, and its main anti-tank weapon is the tube-launched, [laser-guided](term:lgw) Vikhr missile. It gave the aircraft all-weather and night attack capability and was combat-tested with notable success in Chechnya, but only three prototypes and eight production aircraft were built before the programme was cancelled in 2000 in favour of upgrading existing Su-25s. [[1]](#source-1) The Su-25TM development, also called the Su-39, added a Kopyo radar pod and reduced the aircraft's heat signature fourfold. [[2]](#source-2)

## Wherever there is a ground war

Iraq flew Su-25s in the last year of the Iran–Iraq War, sometimes 15 [sorties](term:sortie) a day per aircraft, and Saddam Hussein awarded all of his Su-25 pilots the country's highest military decoration. In 1991 most of them never got airborne against Coalition air power, and seven fled to Iran. [[1]](#source-1) Since then the Su-25 has fought in Abkhazia, Chechnya, Macedonia, Darfur and Syria, and in 2008 Georgia and Russia flew it against each other, with Russian Su-25s bombing the Tbilisi factory where the type was built. Russia and Ukraine both fly it in the war that began in 2022, and both have lost many. Production ended in Georgia in 2010. [[1]](#source-1)
