## A trainer that thinks it is a fighter

The Yak-52 was built to start pilots on the way to jets. It was designed for DOSAAF, the Soviet organisation that trained sport pilots and future military pilots, and it descends from the single-seat Yak-50 aerobatic competition aircraft. [[1]](#source-1) Much of it deliberately copies early postwar jet fighters: the tandem cockpits, the instrument panel and seats, the tail, the tricycle undercarriage and even the overall dimensions match the Yak-17 jet trainer. A student who learned on a Yak-52 would feel at home when stepping up to a jet. [[1]](#source-1)

It is all metal and was built, like most Soviet military aircraft, to work in rough conditions with little maintenance. Compressed air does the work that electrics and hydraulics do in Western aircraft: engine starting, undercarriage, flaps and brakes all run from air bottles behind the rear cockpit, topped up by a compressor on the engine. [[1]](#source-1) Even when retracted the wheels stay partly exposed, adding useful drag in a dive and offering some protection if the pilot has to land with the gear up. [[1]](#source-1)

## Every manoeuvre in the book

The Yak-52's nine-cylinder radial engine and fuel system can keep running upside down for up to two minutes. It is stressed for +7 and −5 g, rolls to the right at up to 352 degrees a second, and can fly every manoeuvre in the Aresti catalogue, the international dictionary of aerobatic figures. [[1]](#source-1) It is flown in its own FAI World Aerobatic Yak-52 Competition, and Western owners have fitted many with new instruments, three-bladed propellers and tailwheels. [[1]](#source-1)

## An unarmed drone hunter

Production moved to Romania under a COMECON agreement, and Aerostar built the Iak-52 there from 1977 to 1998. [[1]](#source-1) Nearly half a century after its first flight the Yak-52 found an unexpected military role. In the Russian invasion of Ukraine, Ukrainian Yak-52s have hunted Russian [reconnaissance](term:reconnaissance) drones, shooting down an Orlan-10 over Odesa in April 2024. Its slow stall speed lets it follow a drone closely while a gunner in the rear seat fires at it, and images suggest at least one has downed as many as eight. [[1]](#source-1)
