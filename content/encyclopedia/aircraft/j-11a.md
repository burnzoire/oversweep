## A fighter bought after an embargo

After the Tiananmen Square crackdown of 1989, Western countries embargoed arms sales to China, and Beijing turned to Moscow. It chose the [Su-27](record:su-27) over the [MiG-29](record:mig-29) and became the Flanker's first export customer. [[1]](#source-1) In 1991 China bought 24 Su-27s for about US$1 billion; they arrived in late 1992 and were based at Wuhu, 250 km west of Shanghai, and a second batch followed in 1996. [[3]](#source-3) China had spent years trying to develop a fighter of equivalent capability at home, and US analysts read the purchase as a sign of little confidence in its own industry. [[3]](#source-3)

## Assembled in Shenyang

In 1996 China and Russia agreed that the Shenyang Aircraft Corporation would build up to 200 Su-27SKs from Russian kits, with no right to export them. [[3]](#source-3) Engines, radars and avionics would still come from Russia. The first two Shenyang aircraft, completed in December 1998, were so poorly assembled that Russian help was needed to rebuild them, and only five had been built by 2000. By 2003 the quality was high and the factory was using Chinese-made airframe parts. [[1]](#source-1)

The Chinese-built aircraft was named the J-11, and NATO calls it **Flanker-B+**. [[1]](#source-1) The **J-11A** was a later production batch with new cockpit displays and the ability to fire the R-77 active-radar missile. About 105 J-11s and J-11As were built in all. [[1]](#source-1) Beneath the new displays it is still a [Su-27SK](record:su-27): a large, long-range, fly-by-wire fighter with a cannon and up to ten air-to-air missiles. [[2]](#source-2)

## Flanker becomes "Flaming Dragon"

China wanted to stop depending on Russia. In 2004, reportedly breaking the co-production agreement, it ended licence assembly and moved to the J-11B "Flaming Dragon", with a Chinese radar, glass cockpit and flight controls and, eventually, the Chinese WS-10 engine. By 2011 about 90% of the J-11B's subsystems and parts were reported to be of Chinese design. [[1]](#source-1) The J-11A fleet was upgraded too: in the mid-2010s some aircraft received four Chinese missile approach warning sensors. [[1]](#source-1)

## Close encounters

The Flanker gave China a long-range fighter, with an operational radius of about 1,500 km, that could also strike targets on the ground and at sea. [[3]](#source-3) During exercises in the Taiwan Strait in March 1996, Chinese Su-27s fired rockets and dropped retarded bombs, the first time such bombs had been shown in public. [[3]](#source-3) Its successors have since made headlines of their own: in August 2014 a J-11B intercepted a US Navy P-8 Poseidon over the South China Sea, passing within 20 ft of its wingtip and rolling over the top of it, in what the Pentagon called an unsafe and unprofessional intercept. [[1]](#source-1)
