## The heavy half of the answer

In 1969 the Soviet Union learned of the US Air Force's F-X programme, which produced the [F-15 Eagle](record:f-15c), and realised the new American fighter would outclass anything it had. Its requirement for an advanced frontline fighter proved too demanding and too costly for one aircraft in the numbers needed, so it was split. Mikoyan got the lightweight half, which became the [MiG-29](record:mig-29); Sukhoi got the heavy half, a long-range fighter with sophisticated avionics, heavy armament and high agility, built to take on the [F-14](record:f-14) and [F-15](record:f-15c). [[1]](#source-1) The mid-1970s arrival of the [F-15](record:f-15c) and [F-16](record:f-16) had left Eastern Bloc fighter pilots at a distinct disadvantage; the Su-27 and [MiG-29](record:mig-29) levelled the field. [[2]](#source-2)

## Starting again

Sukhoi's T-10 prototype first flew on 20 May 1977, and Western satellites spotted it at the Ramenskoye test airfield near Zhukovsky, so intelligence analysts called it "Ram-K" before it became NATO's **Flanker**. [[1]](#source-1) Testing went badly. The aerodynamics were unsatisfactory, the second prototype broke up in flight in July 1978 and killed its pilot, and the Mech radar had serious deficiencies. Sukhoi redesigned the aircraft almost from scratch. The new T-10S, completed in 1980, looked only superficially like the T-10, and its early prototypes suffered crashes and structural failures of their own; one landed in 1983 after losing part of a wing and a tail fin. [[1]](#source-1)

What emerged was the Soviet Union's first operational fly-by-wire fighter. Its large, cropped semi-delta wing blends into the fuselage through leading-edge extensions, the two engines sit in separate pods with a tunnel between them that adds lift, and the twin tails stand outboard of the engines. [[1]](#source-1) The fly-by-wire control, low wing loading and powerful controls make it exceptionally agile, controllable even at very low speed and high [angle of attack](term:aoa). [[1]](#source-1)

## Records and the Cobra

A stripped Su-27 prototype, the P-42 "Streak Flanker", was polished bare, had every gap sealed and its engines boosted, and between 1986 and 1988 it took a series of time-to-climb records from the [F-15](record:f-15c) "Streak Eagle". Several still stood in 2019. [[1]](#source-1) Test pilot Viktor Pugachev first demonstrated the **Cobra** manoeuvre in an Su-27 in 1989: the fighter pitches up past the vertical to an [angle of attack](term:aoa) of about 120 degrees, holds level flight for a moment, then drops its nose again. [[1]](#source-1) The Su-27 and its family set more than 40 world records in all. [[2]](#source-2)

## On guard

The Su-27 joined Soviet units from June 1985, although production difficulties kept it from appearing in strength until 1990. [[1]](#source-1) Its job was long-range [air defence](term:air-defense): to meet American B-1B and B-52 bombers, to protect the coast from aircraft carriers, and to escort Soviet heavy bombers far from home. Its very large internal fuel load gives it a range of about 3,530 km without tanks. [[1]](#source-1)

Some of its intercepts have come too close. On 13 September 1987 an armed Su-27 intercepting a Norwegian P-3 Orion over the Barents Sea collided with it on its third close pass; both aircraft landed safely. In March 2023 a Russian Su-27 dumped fuel on an American MQ-9 Reaper drone over the Black Sea and struck its propeller, forcing the operators to ditch it. [[1]](#source-1)

## A family of Flankers

The Su-27 became the parent of a whole family: the two-seat Su-30, the carrier-borne [Su-33](record:su-33), the side-by-side Su-34 strike aircraft and the Su-35, and China builds its own version as the [J-11](record:j-11a). [[1]](#source-1) Ethiopian Su-27s shot down at least two Eritrean [MiG-29s](record:mig-29) in 1999. Ukraine inherited about 66 to 70 Su-27s in 1991 and has flown them against Russia since 2022; on 7 May 2022 a pair made a high-speed, low-level bombing run on Russian-held Snake Island, filmed by a drone. [[1]](#source-1)
