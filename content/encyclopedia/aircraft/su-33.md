## A navy without a real fighter

Through the 1970s the Soviet Navy's only fixed-wing carrier combat aircraft was the Yak-38 jump jet, whose short range and small payload hobbled its carriers. The navy wanted a bigger ship that could fly conventional fighters, and after several abandoned carrier designs it settled on a larger version of its existing class, to carry navalised MiG-29s and Su-27s. Pilots began training at a new shore facility in Crimea called NITKA, while a steam catapult, arresting gear and landing aids were developed for the ship. [[1]](#source-1)

Then, in 1981, the government cancelled the catapults to save money. The fighters would have to launch under their own power. NITKA was given a take-off ramp, test flights showed the ramp needed a ski-jump profile, and Sukhoi's Su-27K, the aircraft that became the Su-33, was designed around it. [[1]](#source-1)

## Viktor Pugachyov's landings

The first Su-27K prototype flew on 17 August 1987, piloted by Viktor Pugachyov, the test pilot who also first showed the Su-27's Cobra manoeuvre. On 1 November 1989 he became the first Russian pilot to make a conventional landing on an aircraft carrier, aboard the ship then called *Tbilisi* and later renamed *Admiral Kuznetsov*. [[1]](#source-1)

The first launches did not go smoothly. The ship's jet blast deflectors stood too close to the engine nozzles and had to be lowered to 45 degrees, and when the fighter sat in front of one for longer than six seconds its water pipes burst. Pugachyov throttled back, the restraining blocks accidentally retracted, and the fighter crept forward until it was stopped. He then took off without either deflector or blocks, and from then on a Ka-27 rescue helicopter flew near the carrier during flight operations. In the following three weeks the test team flew 227 sorties and made 35 deck landings. [[1]](#source-1)

## Built for the deck

Turning the Su-27 into a carrier fighter meant a stronger structure and undercarriage for no-flare landings, where the aircraft slams onto the deck without slowing its descent. Bigger slats, flaperons and double-slotted flaps add 10–12% of wing area for low-speed handling, and the wings and tailplanes fold to save space below deck. Canards in front of the wing shorten the take-off run, the tail cone is cut back so it does not strike the deck in nose-high landings, and an in-flight refuelling probe and more powerful engines were added. [[1]](#source-1) It can fly as slowly as 240 km/h, and it carries more than twice the fuel of the rival MiG-29K, which lets it fly 80% further at altitude. [[1]](#source-1)

## Two dozen Flanker-Ds

The collapse of the Soviet Union shrank the navy. Had the planned carriers been completed, 72 production aircraft would have been built; only 24 were. *Admiral Kuznetsov* took 13 of them on her first Mediterranean cruise in the winter of 1995–96, and the type formally entered service on 31 August 1998 as the Su-33, NATO's **Flanker-D**. [[1]](#source-1)

Its first combat came on 15 November 2016, when Su-33s flying from *Admiral Kuznetsov* struck targets in Syria's Idlib and Homs provinces, using a newly fitted SVP-24 sighting system that let them drop unguided bombs with near-guided accuracy. Less than three weeks later a Su-33 was lost when an arresting cable snapped on its second landing attempt; the pilot ejected unhurt, and the carrier's aircraft moved ashore to a Syrian air base to continue operations. [[1]](#source-1) China acquired one of the Su-27K prototypes from Ukraine, and its J-15 carrier fighter is widely believed to be directly related to it. [[1]](#source-1)
