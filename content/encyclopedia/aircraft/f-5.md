## Small on purpose

Edgar Schmued had already designed two of the most famous American fighters, the P-51 Mustang and the F-86 Sabre, when he moved to Northrop. In late 1954 his team toured Europe and Asia to see what NATO and SEATO allies actually needed, and Schmued came back with a goal that ran against the trend of the time: instead of making fighters ever bigger and heavier, build one that was small, agile, reliable and cheap, with room to grow so it could serve for more than ten years. [[1]](#source-1)

The key was an engine. General Electric's tiny J85 had been designed to power a decoy missile carried by B-52 bombers, and for its size it was remarkably powerful. Two of them, buried in a slim fuselage, gave Northrop its design. [[1]](#source-1) Chief engineer Welko Gasich added an idea that was new in fighter design, "life cycle cost": designing from the start for low running costs over a long life. [[1]](#source-1)

The Air Force bought the two-seat version first, as the T-38 Talon trainer. [[2]](#source-2) The single-seat fighter nearly died, until the Kennedy administration wanted a low-cost fighter it could supply to allies and chose it in April 1962 as the F-5A Freedom Fighter. [[1]](#source-1) The F-5A was optimised for ground attack and had no fire-control radar. [[2]](#source-2)

## Skoshi Tiger

In October 1965 the US Air Force sent twelve F-5As to Vietnam for a combat evaluation code-named Skoshi Tiger, after fitting them with air-refuelling probes and armour as F-5Cs. They flew more than 2,600 sorties from Bien Hoa and Da Nang, and were judged as capable at ground attack as the F-100, though with shorter range. The trial was as much about selling F-5s abroad as about Air Force service, and in 1967 the surviving aircraft were handed to the South Vietnamese air force. [[1]](#source-1) The name "Tiger" stuck. [[2]](#source-2)

## Tiger II

In 1970 Northrop won the International Fighter Aircraft competition for a successor that could take on the Soviet MiG-21. [[1]](#source-1) The F-5E Tiger II, first flown on 11 August 1972, had more powerful J85-21 engines, a longer fuselage holding more fuel, enlarged leading-edge extensions for better turning, and at last a radar. It kept the F-5A's two M39 cannons in the nose. [[1]](#source-1) Northrop built 792 F-5Es, and more were made under licence in Switzerland, South Korea and Taiwan. [[1]](#source-1)

## The enemy in training

The F-5E never fought for the US Air Force, but it spent its American career playing the enemy. Its size and performance were close to the MiG-21's, so from 1975 to 1990 the Air Force flew it with aggressor squadrons at Nellis in Nevada, in England and in the Philippines, teaching its fighter pilots what a small, agile opponent looked like. [[1]](#source-1) In the 1977 AIMVAL/ACEVAL trials at Nellis, F-14s and F-15s flew against F-5s carrying the new all-aspect AIM-9L; by one account the bigger fighters were "hopelessly outclassed and demoralized" for the first three weeks. [[1]](#source-1)

The US Navy's Fighter Weapons School, TOPGUN, flew F-5Es as adversaries, and the Marines bought used Air Force F-5s in 1989 for the same job. [[1]](#source-1) In the 1990s the Navy moved more adversary flying to the F-5, which cost about a third as much to operate as an F/A-18. [[2]](#source-2) In 2006 it bought 36 low-hour F-5E/Fs from Switzerland and modernised them as F-5Ns, and in 2024 the Marine Corps began collecting 22 more retired Swiss Tigers. One Navy two-seater, nicknamed the "FrankenTiger", was made by grafting the front of an old F-5F onto the back of a newer Swiss F-5E. [[1]](#source-1)

More than 3,800 F-5s and closely related T-38s were built, most of the fighters for American allies. The design also had a famous descendant: a series of Northrop studies that started from the F-5 led to the YF-17 and then the F/A-18 Hornet. [[1]](#source-1)
