## Supersonic, and dangerous

On 25 May 1953 North American's chief test pilot, George Welch, took the YF-100A up for its first flight, seven months ahead of schedule. Levelling off at 35,000 ft, he opened the throttle and went [supersonic](term:supersonic), leaving his chase pilot in an [F-86D](record:f-86) far behind. The F-100 became the first US Air Force fighter able to fly faster than sound in level flight, and the first of the "Century Series" fighters. [[1]](#source-1)

It had begun in 1949 as the "Sabre 45", a company study for an [F-86](record:f-86) with wings swept at 45 degrees, and the Air Force was in such a hurry that it ordered the fighter in quantity before it had flown. [[1]](#source-1) The haste showed. The F-100 suffered from yaw instability that could produce inertia coupling: a sudden yaw and roll, too fast for the pilot to correct, that could overstress the airframe until it broke up. On 12 October 1954 Welch himself was killed when an early F-100A came apart during a dive test. After six major accidents in its first weeks of service, the whole fleet was grounded from November 1954 to February 1955. [[1]](#source-1) Even when fixed, the F-100 could bite: at low speed the wingtips could lose lift and pitch the nose violently up, a deadly trait near the ground that pilots called the "Sabre dance". [[1]](#source-1)

## From day fighter to fighter-bomber

Tactical Air Command wanted a fighter-bomber that could deliver nuclear weapons, which led to the F-100C and then the definitive **F-100D**, a ground-attack aircraft with fighter capability as a secondary role. The D gained an autopilot and better avionics, a longer wing and a much larger fin, and first flew on 24 January 1956. [[1]](#source-1) It entered service with the 405th Fighter Wing at Langley on 29 September 1956. [[1]](#source-1) The two-seat F-100F was a [supersonic](term:supersonic) trainer. [[1]](#source-1)

The "Hun", from "one hundred", collected firsts. An F-100C set a [supersonic](term:supersonic) world speed record in 1955; in December 1956 two F-100Ds made the first ever buddy [air refuelling](term:ar), one fighter passing fuel to another; and in 1959 two F-100Fs became the first jet fighters to fly over the North Pole. [[1]](#source-1) The Thunderbirds flew Super Sabres from 1956 to 1968. [[1]](#source-1)

## The longest war

Six F-100s reached Thailand on 16 April 1961, the first US combat jets of the Vietnam War, and the Hun stayed until July 1971, longer than any other US jet fighter-bomber in that war. [[1]](#source-1) On 4 April 1965, escorting F-105s against the Thanh Hoa Bridge, Captain Donald Kilgus shot at a MiG-17 with his cannon in the US Air Force's first jet air combat of the war, recorded as a probable kill. [[1]](#source-1) F-100s also flew [combat air patrol](term:cap), forward air control and Wild Weasel missions over North Vietnam before moving to [close air support](term:cas) in the South. [[1]](#source-1)

There they worked hardest. Air National Guard [squadrons](term:squadron) sent to Vietnam were among the best: the Air Force commander there later said five of the finest F-100 [squadrons](term:squadron) came from the Guard. [[1]](#source-1) Two-seat F-100Fs flew as "Misty" fast [forward air controllers](term:fac) over North Vietnam and Laos, spotting targets for other fighter-bombers. [[1]](#source-1) By the end the Hun had flown 360,283 combat [sorties](term:sortie); its four fighter wings flew more combat [sorties](term:sortie) in Vietnam than over 15,000 [P-51](record:p-51d) Mustangs had flown in the Second World War. 242 F-100s were lost. [[1]](#source-1) It remained the Air Force's main [close air support](term:cas) aircraft over South Vietnam until the A-7 Corsair II, the F-111 and the [F-4 Phantom](record:f-4) took over. [[1]](#source-1)
