## The jet that flew before it was meant to

On 20 January 1974 the YF-16 was only supposed to be taxiing. Test pilot Phil Oestricher was running it fast down the runway at Edwards when a roll oscillation set in: a fin of the left wingtip missile and then the right tailplane scraped the concrete, and the little jet began to veer off the runway. Rather than risk a crash, Oestricher lifted off and stayed airborne for six minutes before landing safely. The slight damage was quickly repaired, and the "official" first flight went ahead on schedule on 2 February. [[1]](#source-1)

## The Fighter Mafia's fighter

The F-16 began as a protest. Vietnam had shown the US Air Force that it needed agile [air superiority](term:air-superiority) fighters and better-trained fighter pilots. Colonel John Boyd, a Korean War pilot and fighter tactics instructor, worked with the mathematician Thomas Christie on energy–maneuverability theory, which asks how well an aircraft keeps its energy in a fight. Boyd's conclusion was a small, light fighter with a high thrust-to-weight ratio, and the group of like-minded officers around him became known as the "Fighter Mafia". [[1]](#source-1)

Their Lightweight Fighter programme asked for a 20,000 lb class day fighter optimised for the speeds and heights where studies predicted air combat would really happen. General Dynamics' YF-16 flew off against Northrop's twin-engined YF-17. Pilots who flew both chose the YF-16 unanimously: it was markedly quicker going from one manoeuvre to the next. It won the Air Combat Fighter competition on 13 January 1975, helped by its lower operating cost and by sharing the [F-15](record:f-15c)'s F100 engine. The YF-17 did not die; the Navy chose it as the basis of the [F/A-18 Hornet](record:fa-18c). [[1]](#source-1)

Four NATO air forces, Belgium, Denmark, the Netherlands and Norway, were looking for a replacement for their F-104G Starfighters. At the 1975 Paris Air Show they ordered 348 F-16s, built on production lines in the Netherlands and Belgium. Licensed lines later followed in Turkey and South Korea. [[1]](#source-1)

## "You don't fly an F-16; it flies you"

The F-16 was the first production fighter deliberately designed to be slightly unstable. That relaxed stability cuts drag and sharpens the turn, but no human could fly it unaided, so a four-channel fly-by-wire system makes thousands of corrections a second and keeps the jet inside its angle-of-attack and g limits. With no mechanical linkage at all between stick and control surfaces, early pilots called it "the electric jet". [[1]](#source-1) It was also the first fighter built from the outset to pull 9 g. [[1]](#source-1) Earlier fighters were designed for about 7 g, because it was believed a pilot could not handle more even with a g-suit. [[2]](#source-2)

Everything about the cockpit serves the fight. A single-piece, frameless bubble canopy gives an all-round view, the ACES II seat is tilted back 30 degrees to help the pilot tolerate g, and the stick sits on the right-hand armrest. The first side-sticks did not move at all; pilots found them hard to fly precisely, so later sticks were given a little play. [[1]](#source-1) The wing blends into the fuselage, adding lift and making room for fuel. [[2]](#source-2)

It was named the Fighting Falcon on 21 July 1980, but almost nobody uses the name. Crews call it the **Viper**, after the snake and after the starfighters of *Battlestar Galactica*, which was on television when the F-16 entered service. [[1]](#source-1)

## From dogfighter to workhorse

The F-16C and two-seat F-16D entered production in 1984. The first of them, the Block 25, brought the AN/APG-68 radar and with it all-weather, beyond-visual-range fighting with the AIM-7 and AIM-120. The blocks that followed were the 30/32, 40/42 and 50/52; the second number of each pair marks the Pratt & Whitney-engined version. [[1]](#source-1) An M61A1 cannon sits in the left wing root, and missiles, bombs, tanks and pods hang from the wingtip rails and nine more stations. [[1]](#source-1)

Built as a dogfighter, the F-16 spent most of its US career dropping bombs. It replaced the F-105, A-7 and [F-4](record:f-4), flew strike and [suppression of enemy air defences](term:sead) ([SEAD](term:sead)) missions, and by 1996 had taken over the Wild Weasel role from the [F-4G](record:f-4). [[1]](#source-1)

## In combat

Israel drew first blood. On 28 April 1981 an Israeli F-16 shot down a Syrian [Mi-8](record:mi-8mtv2) helicopter with its gun over the Bekaa Valley, and on 7 June 1981 eight F-16s, escorted by F-15s, struck the Osirak reactor near Baghdad. In the 1982 Lebanon War, during one of the largest jet air battles ever fought, Israeli F-16s were credited with 44 air-to-air kills. [[1]](#source-1)

US Air Force, Reserve and Air National Guard F-16s flew in Desert Storm in 1991, over the Balkans, in the Iraqi no-fly zones, and in Afghanistan, Iraq and Libya. [[1]](#source-1) Not every mission went their way: on 2 May 1999 an F-16 flown by David Goldfein, later the Air Force's Chief of Staff, was shot down over western Serbia by a surface-to-air missile brigade. [[1]](#source-1) On 11 September 2001 two unarmed F-16s were launched to ram and bring down the hijacked United Airlines Flight 93; the airliner crashed before they reached it, after its passengers stormed the cockpit. [[1]](#source-1)

US F-16s also fly with the Thunderbirds demonstration team and as aggressor aircraft for the US Navy. With 25 other air forces flying it as well, it is the most numerous fixed-wing aircraft in military service, and in 2022 the Air Force announced it would keep its F-16s flying for another two decades. [[1]](#source-1)
