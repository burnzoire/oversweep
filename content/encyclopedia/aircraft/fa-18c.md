## Forty seconds over Iraq

On 17 January 1991, the first day of the Gulf War, four Navy Hornets launched from USS *Saratoga* in the Red Sea to bomb an Iraqi airfield. Each carried four 2,000 lb bombs. On the way in, an E-2C radar aircraft warned them of approaching Iraqi MiG-21s. Lieutenant Commander Mark Fox and Lieutenant Nick Mongilio shot both MiGs down with Sparrow and Sidewinder missiles; just 40 seconds passed between the MiGs appearing on the E-2's radar and both going down. Then the Hornets carried on, bombed their target and flew back to the carrier. [[1]](#source-1) That one sortie is the whole idea of the aircraft: a fighter and an attack aircraft in the same airframe, hence the name **F/A-18**. [[1]](#source-1)

## The loser that won

The Hornet began as a runner-up. Northrop's twin-engined YF-17 lost the US Air Force Lightweight Fighter competition to the YF-16. [[1]](#source-1) But the Navy, ordered by Congress to find something cheaper than the F-14, doubted that a single-engined fighter on narrow landing gear could be adapted to carrier life, and on 2 May 1975 it picked the YF-17 instead. [[1]](#source-1) McDonnell Douglas and Northrop then rebuilt it for the sea: more fuel, larger wings, a wider rear fuselage and the first fully digital fly-by-wire system in a production fighter. On 1 March 1977 the Secretary of the Navy named it the Hornet. [[1]](#source-1)

The first F/A-18A was rolled out on 13 September 1978, painted with "Navy" on one side and "Marines" on the other, because both services would fly it. [[1]](#source-1) It entered service with Marine squadron VMFA-314 on 7 January 1983, and with the Navy in 1984, replacing F-4 Phantoms and A-7 Corsairs. Early reports said the Hornet was extraordinarily reliable, a big change from the Phantom. [[1]](#source-1)

## One pilot, two jobs

The Hornet was designed around versatile avionics, multifunction cockpit displays and excellent handling at high angles of attack. [[1]](#source-1) In the F/A-18C, the APG-73 radar and targeting pods let a single pilot switch between air-to-air and air-to-ground work on the same sortie, and an M61A1 gun sits in the nose. [[1]](#source-1) It can fly fighter escort, fleet air defence, suppression of enemy air defences, interdiction, close air support and reconnaissance. [[1]](#source-1) The two-seat F/A-18D carries either a weapon systems officer or a second set of controls for training. [[2]](#source-2)

## In action

Hornets first saw combat over Libya in 1986. [[1]](#source-1) In the Gulf War the Navy and Marine Corps deployed 190 of them, which flew 4,551 sorties. One Hornet took hits in both engines and still flew 125 miles back to base; it was flying again within days. Three Navy Hornets were lost, and on the first night of the war Lieutenant Commander Scott Speicher of VFA-81 was shot down and killed, probably by a missile from an Iraqi MiG-25. [[1]](#source-1) Hornets went on to fight over Bosnia and Kosovo and in the 2003 Iraq War. [[1]](#source-1)

For more than three decades the Navy's Blue Angels flew Hornets in their displays, having switched from the A-4 Skyhawk in 1986. [[1]](#source-1) The Navy replaced its Hornets with the larger Super Hornet and the F-35C, and the last operational Navy F/A-18C flight took place on 2 October 2019. [[1]](#source-1) The Marine Corps kept going: in 2024 it still flew 179 Hornets, with upgraded radars and electronics, planned to serve until about 2030 as the F-35 replaces them. Several other air forces also fly the Hornet. [[1]](#source-1)
