## "A dinosaur with good proportions"

The Thunderbolt was huge. Its designer, Alexander Kartveli, had fled his native Georgia after the Bolshevik revolution, and when combat reports from Europe in 1940 showed that his company's lightweight fighter designs were outclassed, he drew something nobody had imagined: the largest single-engined fighter of the war, built around the most powerful air-cooled radial engine available and armed with eight .50 calibre machine guns. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) Empty, the prototype weighed 65% more than Republic's previous fighter. "It will be a dinosaur," Kartveli said, "but it will be a dinosaur with good proportions." [[1]](#source-1)

To make it fast at high altitude, Kartveli fitted a turbosupercharger in the rear fuselage, driven by exhaust pipes that ran past the cockpit and glowed red at full power. The ductwork was so important that it was designed first and the rest of the aircraft built around it; it filled nearly the whole belly. After the fighter entered service, crash investigators found that those ducts formed a cushion between the pilot and the ground. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The cockpit was roomy and air-conditioned, with a seat one pilot compared to a lounge chair. [[1]](#source-1) The XP-47B first flew on 6 May 1941 and later reached 412 mph in level flight. [[1]](#source-1)

## The Jug

P-47s reached England at the end of 1942, and pilots soon called it the **Jug**, because its profile looked like a milk jug. Major Don Blakeslee scored its first air victory, over an Fw 190, on 15 April 1943. [[1]](#source-1) The 56th Fighter Group became the Eighth Air Force's top-scoring group and the only one that kept its Thunderbolts, by choice, when the others switched to [Mustangs](record:p-51d). Its pilots claimed 677½ aircraft in the air; among them Francis Gabreski scored 28 victories and Robert S. Johnson 27. [[1]](#source-1) Luftwaffe ace Heinz Bär said the P-47 "could absorb an astounding amount of lead and had to be handled very carefully". [[1]](#source-1)

In the Pacific, Colonel Neel Kearby of the 348th Fighter Group led four P-47s into 40 Japanese aircraft over Wewak, New Guinea, on 11 October 1943 and shot down six himself, earning the Medal of Honor. [[1]](#source-1)

## The fighter-bomber

Its greatest work was close to the ground. Pilots returning from escort missions shot up targets of opportunity, and the Thunderbolt's tough airframe and air-cooled engine could take damage that would have brought down a liquid-cooled fighter. It became the Army Air Forces' main fighter-bomber, carrying bombs of up to 1,000 lb and, from 1944, 5 in rockets nicknamed "Holy Moses". [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) Between D-Day and the end of the war in Europe, P-47 pilots claimed 86,000 railway cars, 9,000 locomotives, 6,000 armoured vehicles and 68,000 trucks. On 29 July 1944, during the Normandy breakout, the 405th Fighter Group spent more than six hours attacking a trapped German column near Roncey; investigators on the ground later counted 66 tanks, 204 vehicles and 11 guns destroyed. [[1]](#source-1) Across the war the Thunderbolt flew more than 746,000 [sorties](term:sortie) with an air-to-air kill ratio of 4.6 to 1. [[1]](#source-1)

## Many flags

Other nations flew the Jug too. RAF Thunderbolts attacked the Japanese in Burma; Brazil's 1st Fighter Squadron flew 445 missions over Italy; and Mexico's 201st Fighter Squadron flew 791 [sorties](term:sortie) in the Philippines without losing a pilot or aircraft to the enemy. [[1]](#source-1) The Free French received 446, and the Soviet Union tested and used about 200 for high-altitude [air defence](term:air-defense). [[1]](#source-1) After the war the Air Force renamed it the F-47 and kept it with the Air National Guard until 1953, while Latin American air forces flew it into the 1960s. [[1]](#source-1)
