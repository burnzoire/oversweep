## A fighter that learned to bomb

The Eagle's designers had promised "not a pound for air-to-ground", yet the [F-15](record:f-15c) had long been seen as having real potential as a bomber. The Strike Eagle, which arrived in 1987, turned that potential into a mission statement as blunt as the fighter's: put bombs on target, deep behind enemy lines. [[2]](#source-2)

It began as a company gamble. McDonnell Douglas converted the second two-seat [F-15](record:f-15c) prototype into a demonstrator, which first flew on 8 July 1980, trying out conformal fuel tanks and a laser designator pod before the Air Force had asked for anything. In 1981 the Air Force did ask, for a long-range strike aircraft to replace the F-111 that could fight its way in without escorts or [jammers](term:electromagnetic-jamming). The rival was General Dynamics' F-16XL, a striking cranked-delta [F-16](record:f-16). On 24 February 1984 the Air Force chose the F-15E: it was cheaper to develop, had more room to grow, and had two engines. The first production F-15E flew on 31 March 1987. [[1]](#source-1)

## Two seats, darker paint

You can tell a Strike Eagle from other Eagles at a glance: darker grey paint, the conformal tanks hugging its fuselage, navigation and targeting pods under its intakes, and a second seat. [[1]](#source-1) The pilot flies and fights; the weapon systems officer (WSO) behind works the radar, the targeting pods and the weapons. [[1]](#source-1)

Its radar maps the ground in fine detail for attacks at night and in bad weather, and LANTIRN pods gave it low-level navigation and laser targeting in the dark. Later aircraft carry the APG-82 AESA radar and newer targeting pods. [[1]](#source-1) A typical load mixes guided bombs or Mavericks with AIM-9 and AIM-120 missiles for self-defence. [[2]](#source-2) Heavier strike loads meant the engine bay was designed to take more powerful engines, and later batches have F100-PW-229s of about 29,000 lbf each. [[1]](#source-1) It is also the US Air Force's most capable tactical nuclear delivery aircraft, able to carry up to five B61 Mod 12 bombs. [[1]](#source-1)

## Scud hunting

The Strike Eagle went to war barely three years after entering service. On the first night of Desert Storm, 17 January 1991, 24 F-15Es struck five fixed Scud sites in western Iraq. For the rest of the war they flew night hunter missions over the western desert, searching for mobile Scud launchers. Two were lost in the first days, one over the heavily defended Basrah area and one to an [SA-2](record:sa-2). [[1]](#source-1)

On 14 February a Strike Eagle crew answering a call for help from US Special Forces scored the jet's only air-to-air kill of the war in the most unusual way. Through their infrared sensor they spotted an Iraqi [Mi-24](record:mi-24p) helicopter unloading troops and dropped a 2,000 lb [laser-guided](term:lgw) GBU-10 bomb on it. The crew thought they had missed and were reaching for a Sidewinder when the helicopter exploded; the Special Forces team estimated it was about 800 ft above the ground when the bomb hit. [[1]](#source-1)

After the war, enforcing the no-fly zone over northern Iraq, a flight of F-15Es watched Iraqi helicopters attack Kurdish refugees but were not allowed to fire. Instead they flew high-speed passes as close as they could, buffeting the helicopters with wake turbulence and shining lasers at their cockpits, and one Hind crashed. [[1]](#source-1)

## Thirty years at war

Since then the Strike Eagle has flown over the Balkans, Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, Syria and Iran, on deep strikes, [combat air patrols](term:cap) and [close air support](term:cas) for troops on the ground. [[1]](#source-1) Its roles keep widening: on 13 April 2024 US F-15Es helped blunt an Iranian attack on Israel, shooting down more than 70 one-way attack drones. [[1]](#source-1)

Israel flies it as the F-15I Ra'am ("Thunder"), and Saudi Arabia, South Korea, Singapore and Qatar have their own versions. In US service the F-15E is expected to fly into the 2030s, and the F-15EX Eagle II carries the line on. [[1]](#source-1)
