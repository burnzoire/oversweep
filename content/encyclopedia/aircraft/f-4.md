## The triumph of thrust over aerodynamics

On 6 December 1959 Commander Lawrence Flint of the US Navy took the second Phantom prototype to [Mach](term:mach-number) 2.5 at 47,000 ft, pulled up into a 45-degree climb, shut the engines down as the air thinned, and coasted on upward to a world record height of 98,557 ft. On the way back down he relit the engines and flew home. It was one of 16 world records the Phantom set to show off the Navy's new fighter; five of its speed records stood until the [F-15](record:f-15c) arrived in 1975. [[1]](#source-1)

The Phantom was big, heavy and brutally powerful. It was designed as a fleet-defence interceptor: climb fast, fly high and fast, and shoot down attackers with radar-guided missiles at long range. Like other interceptors of its day it had no gun. [[1]](#source-1) It entered Navy service in 1961 and was soon taken up by the US Marine Corps and the US Air Force, an unusual thing for a Navy fighter at the time. With 5,195 built, it is the most-produced American [supersonic](term:supersonic) military aircraft. [[1]](#source-1) Its crews' nicknames say a lot about it: "Rhino", "Double Ugly", "the Flying Brick", and "the triumph of thrust over aerodynamics". [[1]](#source-1)

## Speed is life

Vietnam put the Phantom into the kind of fight it had not been built for. Rules of engagement usually demanded that pilots identify a target by eye before firing, which threw away the advantage of long-range missiles, and the missiles of the day were often unreliable. Fights turned into close, slow dogfights, where the agile MiGs could usually out-turn the heavy Phantom. [[1]](#source-1) Phantom pilots' answer was their slogan, "Speed is life": the F-4's acceleration and thrust let a skilled pilot join or leave a fight at will. [[1]](#source-1) Many still wanted a gun. "Bullets are cheap and tend to go where you aim them," said John Chesire, who flew Phantoms from carriers. "I needed a gun, and I really wished I had one." [[1]](#source-1)

The Air Force's **F-4E**, first delivered in October 1967, gave them one: an internal 20 mm multibarrel gun with a better fire-control system, plus an extra fuselage fuel tank and an improved engine. [[2]](#source-2) In 1972 it gained leading-edge slats, which greatly improved handling at high angles of attack at some cost in top speed. [[1]](#source-1)

## MiG killers

Phantom crews of all three services fought through the air war over Vietnam. On 2 January 1967 Colonel Robin Olds led the Air Force's 8th Tactical Fighter Wing in Operation Bolo: his F-4Cs flew from Thailand mimicking a strike force of F-105 bombers, and the North Vietnamese [MiG-21s](record:mig-21) that rose to meet them found Phantoms instead. North Vietnam lost half of its [MiG-21](record:mig-21) fleet without a single American loss. [[1]](#source-1) On 10 May 1972 the Navy's Randy Cunningham and William Driscoll shot down three MiG-17s in one fight to become the first American aces of the war, and that August Captain Steve Ritchie became the Air Force's first. [[1]](#source-1) The Phantom remains the last aircraft in which a member of any US service has become an ace, and its admirers called it the "World's Leading Distributor of MiG Parts". [[1]](#source-1)

It was not one-sided. North Vietnam's lighter [MiG-21s](record:mig-21) took a steady toll, and the Air Force lost 528 F-4s and RF-4Cs in the war; with Navy and Marine Corps losses the total was 761 Phantoms. [[1]](#source-1)

## Pave Tack and the Wild Weasel

From 1973, Air Force F-4Es received a zoom-lens TV camera for identifying aircraft and ground targets at long range, and Pave Tack, a pod that let the crew find, track and laser-designate ground targets by day or night. A digital computer worked out launch solutions for the Sidewinder and Sparrow. [[2]](#source-2) The F-4E carries four AIM-7 Sparrows semi-recessed under its fuselage and AIM-9 Sidewinders on the wing pylons. [[1]](#source-1)

Its final US combat job was the most dangerous. The **F-4G** Wild Weasel, an F-4E with [electronic warfare](term:ew) equipment in place of its gun, hunted surface-to-air missile sites in Desert Storm, often paired with an [F-16](record:f-16). The last Air Force combat Phantoms, F-4Gs of the Idaho Air National Guard, retired after a deployment that ended in December 1995. [[2]](#source-2) At sea, the last Phantom carrier landing was made by a Naval Reserve F-4S on 18 October 1986. [[1]](#source-1)

The last of all 5,195 Phantoms was built in Japan by Mitsubishi on 21 May 1981, and a few Phantoms remain in service abroad. [[1]](#source-1) The aircraft's emblem, a cartoon ghost called "The Spook", still turns up wherever it has flown. [[1]](#source-1)
