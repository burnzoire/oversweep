## Through the sound barrier, level

The [MiG-15](record:mig-15) and MiG-17 could only pass the speed of sound in a dive. In 1950 the Mikoyan-Gurevich bureau began a fighter with more range that could do it in level flight, and chose to power it with two new, small axial-flow engines. A MiG-17 converted to take the pair of engines flew late in 1951 as a test bed, and the real prototype, the SM-2, flew on 24 May 1952 with G. A. Sedov at the controls. Its thin wings, designed at the TsAGI research institute, were swept back at 55 degrees. [[1]](#source-1)

The SM-2 still could not go [supersonic](term:supersonic) in level flight, even with [afterburning](term:afterburner) engines, and it tended to spin at high angles of attack. The designers lowered the tailplane, deepened the wing fences and moved the air brakes, and the Mikulin bureau produced a stronger engine, the AM-9B, later known as the Tumansky RD-9. Fitted with it, the aircraft flew as the SM-9 on 5 January 1954 and so impressed the authorities that it was ordered into production as the MiG-19 on 17 February 1954, although factory testing had barely begun. [[1]](#source-1) It became the first Soviet production aircraft able to fly faster than sound in level flight. [[1]](#source-1)

## A rushed start

That haste had a price. Early MiG-19s suffered mid-air explosions, eventually traced to poor insulation between the engines and the fuel tanks in the rear fuselage; a metal heat shield partly cured them. The elevators lost their effect at [supersonic](term:supersonic) speed, so the main production day fighter, the **MiG-19S**, was given an all-moving tailplane. A radar-equipped all-weather fighter was developed alongside it and entered production in 1955 as the **MiG-19P**, with an Izumrud radar and its guns in the wing roots; from 1957 the missile-armed MiG-19PM replaced its cannon with four K-5M missiles. [[1]](#source-1)

The Soviet public first saw the type on 3 July 1955, when 48 MiG-19s flew past at the Tushino air show in Moscow. [[1]](#source-1) Its two engines breathe through what looks like a single round intake in the nose but is divided inside, so each engine has its own duct. [[2]](#source-2)

## Chasing spy planes

Soviet MiG-19s spent the late 1950s intercepting Western [reconnaissance](term:reconnaissance) flights. In the autumn of 1957 a MiG-19 pilot saw a U-2 but could not climb the last 3,000 m to reach it, and a lightened high-altitude version, the MiG-19SV, still fell short. In 1960, when Francis Gary Powers's U-2 was brought down by surface-to-air missiles, the same salvo hit a pursuing MiG-19P and killed its pilot, Sergei Safronov. On 1 July 1960 a MiG-19 shot down an RB-47H over the Arctic in a hotly disputed incident, and in January 1964 one shot down a T-39 Sabreliner that had entered East German airspace on a training flight. [[1]](#source-1)

## China's J-6

The Soviet Union replaced the MiG-19 with the [MiG-21](record:mig-21) in the early 1960s, but the type lived on for decades elsewhere. China built it as the J-6, its first [supersonic](term:supersonic) fighter, which began flight tests in 1958 and was reportedly still in production in the mid-1990s. Including Chinese, Polish and Czechoslovak production, as many as 10,000 MiG-19s of all versions may have been built. [[2]](#source-2) In 1965 a PLAAF J-6 shot down a US Air Force F-104 Starfighter that had flown into Chinese airspace over Hainan Island; its pilot, Captain Philip E. Smith, ejected and was held prisoner until March 1973. [[1]](#source-1)

## The fighter that out-turned the Phantom

Over North Vietnam the 925th Fighter Regiment flew J-6s from 1969. Air combat there was fought almost entirely at [subsonic](term:subsonic) speed, where the older fighter was more manoeuvrable than the [MiG-21](record:mig-21) and accelerated well enough. American pilots who tested an export F-6 in 1969 called it an excellent fighter: "like the MiG-17, it could easily out-turn the [Phantom](record:f-4)...and could out-accelerate the [F-4](record:f-4) out to [Mach](term:mach-number) 1.2". They praised its canopy and its three hard-hitting 30 mm cannon. Its great weakness was range; as one test pilot put it, "after going in full after-burner at low altitude for five minutes, the MiG driver will be looking for a place to land!" Short range and two hard-to-maintain engines made it unpopular with North Vietnamese pilots. [[1]](#source-1)

Egypt flew about 80 MiG-19s by 1967. On 29 November 1966 an Israeli Mirage III downed one with a R.530 missile, the French missile's first aerial kill, and the second with its guns. More than half of Egypt's MiG-19s were destroyed on the ground in the opening strikes of the Six-Day War, but Israeli pilots regarded the type as a dangerous opponent, and in May 1969 a MiG-19 shot down a Mirage with cannon fire. North Korea was still flying MiG-19s in 2014. [[1]](#source-1)
