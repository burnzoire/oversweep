---
short: Flight
usage: Fixed-wing and rotary-wing pilots of every service
terms:
  - term: Adverse yaw
    definition: Yaw opposite to the direction of bank when rolling, caused by the differential drag and the slightly different speeds of the two wings. Coordinated rudder counters it.
    cite: [{ n: 5, page: "6-3" }, { n: 6, page: "5-8" }]
  - term: Autorotation
    definition: Descent with the rotor turned by the upward flow of air instead of the engine. After an engine failure the pilot lowers the collective at once to keep the rotor turning.
    note: "In the autorotating rotor the middle of the blade, normally 25 to 70 percent of the radius, is the driving region; the outer 30 percent is driven, held back by drag; the inner 25 percent is stalled. [7]"
    cite: [{ n: 7, page: "11-2, 2-26" }]
  - term: Camber
    definition: The maximum distance between an airfoil's mean camber line, which runs midway between its upper and lower surfaces, and its chord line.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "33" }]
  - term: Chord line
    definition: The straight line through the leading edge and the trailing edge of an airfoil.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "33" }]
  - term: Corner speed
    definition: The speed where the stall boundary meets the limit load, giving the best instantaneous turn. The civil handbooks call it the maneuvering speed, VA, the maximum speed at which the positive design load limit can be imposed.
    note: "See also corner airspeed in the air combat terms."
    cite: [{ n: 6, page: "5-19" }]
  - term: Critical angle of attack
    definition: The angle of attack at which the lift coefficient reaches its maximum, CLmax. Beyond it the airflow is unable to adhere to the upper surface, lift falls away and the wing stalls.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "50" }, { n: 1, page: "23" }]
  - term: Dissymmetry of lift
    definition: The difference in lift between the advancing and retreating halves of a rotor disk in forward flight. Blade flapping is the primary means of compensating for it.
    cite: [{ n: 7, page: "2-20" }, { n: 4, page: "¶1-32–1-33" }]
  - term: Dynamic rollover
    definition: A roll about a skid or wheel pinned to the ground that carries a helicopter past the point where cyclic can stop it, about 5 to 8 degrees. Quickly reducing collective is the most effective way to stop it.
    cite: [{ n: 7, page: "11-12" }]
  - term: Effective translational lift (ETL)
    definition: The point, between about 16 and 24 knots of forward speed, where the rotor disk completely outruns the recirculation of its own vortices and the same blade pitch gives more lift.
    cite: [{ n: 7, page: "2-21–2-24" }, { n: 4, page: "¶1-24" }]
  - term: Energy height
    form: "(ES)"
    definition: "ES = h + V² ÷ 2g: altitude plus the altitude the airspeed could ideally buy in a zoom, a single measure of an aircraft's total energy."
    cite: [{ n: 6, page: "4-7–4-8" }]
  - term: Excess thrust
    definition: Thrust available minus thrust required. Positive excess thrust causes a climb, an acceleration, or both, depending on angle of attack.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "60–63" }]
  - term: Ground effect
    definition: The surface's interference with the airflow around a wing flying within about one wingspan of it, which reduces induced drag. For a helicopter, the increase in rotor efficiency within about one rotor diameter of the surface, so a hover in ground effect takes less power.
    cite: [{ n: 5, page: "5-11–5-12" }, { n: 7, page: "2-12" }]
  - term: Induced drag
    definition: The portion of total drag associated with the production of lift. In level flight it varies inversely with the square of the airspeed, so it is greatest when slow.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "55–56" }, { n: 5, page: "5-7–5-8" }]
  - term: L/Dmax
    definition: The best lift-to-drag ratio. The angle of attack that gives it produces the minimum total drag.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "57–58" }]
  - term: Lift-limit and load-limit pull
    definition: "The two maximum-performance pulls. Below corner speed the pull is lift-limited, flown to the buffet (19 to 21 units in the T-45); above it the pull is load-limited, flown to the G limit."
    cite: [{ n: 3, page: "4-3" }]
  - term: Load factor
    definition: The ratio of lift to weight, felt as G. In a level, coordinated turn it is 1 ÷ cos(bank), and the stall speed in the turn is the wings-level stall speed times its square root.
    cite: [{ n: 1, page: "37" }]
  - term: Parasite drag
    definition: Drag that is not associated with the production of lift, made up of form drag, friction drag and interference drag. It varies directly with velocity squared.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "52–54" }]
  - term: Retreating blade stall
    definition: Stall of a helicopter's retreating blade at high forward speed, from its high angle of attack and slow relative wind. The first step in recovery is usually to reduce collective.
    cite: [{ n: 7, page: "2-20, 11-11" }]
  - term: Stabilator
    definition: A one-piece moving horizontal tail used instead of a stabilizer and elevator, as on the F/A-18 and F-15.
    cite: [{ n: 2, page: "68" }]
  - term: Units
    form: "(AOA)"
    definition: How the T-45 and other Navy trainers display angle of attack, a type-specific scale rather than degrees of chord-line angle. The T-45 reaches its maximum lift coefficient at 21 units, in heavy buffet.
    usage: Navy aircraft
    cite: [{ n: 3, page: "4-1–4-3" }]
  - term: Vortex ring state
    definition: "Also called settling with power: a vertical or nearly vertical descent of at least 300 feet per minute, with the rotor using some engine power and horizontal speed below ETL, in which the rotor settles into its own downwash. Adding power makes it worse."
    cite: [{ n: 7, page: "11-10" }]

## The words of flight

These are the words of how aircraft fly: the wing and angle of attack, drag, control, turns and energy, and the rotor. Military training texts come first, from the Navy's aerodynamics books and its T-45 fighter syllabus to the Army's fundamentals of flight for helicopter crews. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4)

The FAA's handbooks describe the same physics for civil pilots and are cited where they state a point more directly, above all for helicopter hazards. [[5]](#source-5) [[6]](#source-6) [[7]](#source-7) Angle of attack itself is in the joint terms.
