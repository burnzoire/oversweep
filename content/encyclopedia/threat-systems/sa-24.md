## Overview

The 9K338 Igla-S, NATO reporting name **SA-24 Grinch**, is the latest version of the Igla family of shoulder-fired missiles, after the Igla-1 ([SA-16](record:sa-16)) and the Igla ([SA-18](record:sa-18)). [[1]](#source-1) It has a longer range, a more sensitive seeker, better resistance to countermeasures and a heavier warhead than the Igla, and the manufacturer claims a hit probability of 0.8–0.9. [[1]](#source-1) It entered service in 2002 and has been produced since December 2004. [[1]](#source-1) Since 2014 Russia has been replacing it with the [SA-29](record:sa-29) Verba. [[1]](#source-1)

## How it engages

Like the Igla, it [homes](term:homing) passively on infrared. The Igla family's seeker has a cooled detector to find the target and a second detector to recognise [decoy flares](term:decoy-flare); the Igla-S adds detectors around the main seeker to resist pulsed infrared jammers of the kind fitted to helicopters. [[1]](#source-1) Its warhead is 2.5 kg, against 1.17 kg for the earlier Iglas. [[1]](#source-1) The Strelets mount fires it from vehicles and the Dzhigit from a two-round pedestal. [[1]](#source-1)

## Envelope

The comparison table in the source gives a 6 km (3.2 NM) maximum range, against 5.2 km for the SA-18, and says it can catch a target receding at 400 m/s or approaching at 320 m/s. [[1]](#source-1) It gives no Igla-S ceiling or minimum range, so Figure 1 draws the range only. As for any shoulder-fired missile, the real threat depends on the shooter seeing the aircraft and on its heat signature.

## On the radar warning receiver

A passive infrared missile gives no radar warning. Look for missile warning cues and the launch itself, and use flares and terrain.

## History

In Libya in 2011, loyalist forces fired three Igla-S at British Army Apache helicopters, which evaded them with flares. [[1]](#source-1) In 2015 an Igla-S, reportedly from a truck-mounted Strelets, shot down a MiG-23UB over Al Watiya. [[1]](#source-1) Azerbaijani soldiers shot down an Armenian [Mi-24](record:mi-24p) with one in November 2014, and Armenian forces are reported to have used it against Azerbaijani aircraft and Bayraktar TB2 drones in 2020. [[1]](#source-1) Missiles looted from Libya in 2011 are reported to have reached militants in Gaza and Lebanon, and Syrian rebels were photographed with SA-24s in 2012. [[1]](#source-1)
