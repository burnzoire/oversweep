## Overview

The S-300, NATO reporting name **SA-10 Grumble**, is a family of long-range surface-to-air missile systems built by NPO Almaz for the Soviet Air Defence Forces against air raids and cruise missiles. The P suffix stands for PVO-Strany, country [air defence](term:air-defense). [[1]](#source-1) This record covers the early land-based S-300P versions that carry the SA-10 name:

- **S-300P / S-300PT (SA-10A)**: the original, semi-mobile version. More than 80 systems were active in 1987, mainly around Moscow.
- **S-300PS / S-300PM (SA-10B)**: introduced in 1985 with truck-mounted launchers, radar and command post, and the 5V55R missile.
- **S-300PMU (SA-10C)**: the export version, completed in 1985. [[1]](#source-1)

The later S-300PMU-1 and PMU-2 are SA-20 Gargoyle, the naval S-300F is SA-N-6, and the army S-300V is a different design, [SA-12](record:sa-12). [[1]](#source-1)

## The battery

An S-300PT unit has a 36D6 **Tin Shield** surveillance radar, a 30N6 **Flap Lid** fire-control radar and 5P85-1 semi-trailer launchers, usually with a 76N6 **Clam Shell** low-altitude radar as well. The original S-300P paired the 5N66M Clam Shell continuous-wave acquisition radar with the 30N6 Flap Lid A I/J-band phased-array tracking and engagement radar, plus a command trailer and up to twelve launchers with four missiles each. The S-300PS is similar, with an upgraded 30N6 that includes the command post, and truck-mounted launchers. A regiment typically has three batteries, with the 64N6 **Big Bird** as its search radar. [[1]](#source-1)

The Clam Shell sits on a mast 24–39 m tall and detects a target 500 m above the ground at 90 km. [[1]](#source-1)

## How it engages

The missiles are launched vertically, so they can engage in any direction without turning the launcher; they are catapulted clear of the tube before the motor fires, then tip over towards the target. Early versions are guided by the 30N6 Flap Lid using command guidance with terminal semi-active radar [homing](term:homing). [[1]](#source-1) The original design was meant to use track-via-missile guidance, but that could not track targets below 500 m, so attacking [SEAD](term:sead) aircraft could hide in the terrain. Command guidance for the first part of the flight was added, which brought the minimum engagement altitude down to 25 m. [[1]](#source-1)

The 5V55K missile has a range of 47 km on command guidance. The 5V55R (1981) reaches 75 km, flies at up to 2,000 m/s and carries a 130 kg warhead with proximity and contact [fuzes](term:fuze). [[1]](#source-1)

## Envelope

The S-300PMU (SA-10C) engages aircraft at 5–90 km (2.7–49 NM) and from 25 m to 27 km (82 to 89,000 ft); its 5V55K reaches 45–47 km and its 5V55R 75–90 km. The S-300PS's 5V55R raised the maximum to 75 km. [[1]](#source-1) Figure 1 uses the S-300PMU figures, the largest the source gives for an SA-10 version, so it is not the envelope of every SA-10 site.

## On the radar warning receiver

In the DCS [F-14](record:f-14), the AN/ALR-67 shows the Flap Lid track radar as **10**, the Big Bird search radar as **BB** and the Clam Shell search radar as **CS**. [[3]](#source-3)

## History

The S-300's first use in war was the 2020 Nagorno-Karabakh conflict, where both sides listed it. Ukraine had around 100 active batteries when Russia invaded in February 2022, and Slovakia transferred its only battery to Ukraine in April 2022. Ukrainian officials reported in 2022 that Russia was firing S-300 missiles at ground targets. [[1]](#source-1) Iran's last three S-300 batteries were disabled by Israeli F-35s in October 2024.
