## Overview

The 9K310 Igla-1, NATO reporting name **SA-16 Gimlet**, is a simplified early version of the Igla ([SA-18](record:sa-18)). Development of the Igla began in 1972, and when it ran late the programme split in 1978: the Igla-1, with a simpler seeker based on the Strela-3's ([SA-14](record:sa-14)), would enter service first. The Igla-1 and its 9M313 missile were accepted into Soviet Army service on 11 March 1981. [[1]](#source-1) The export version is the Igla-1E. [[1]](#source-1)

## How it engages

Compared with the Strela-3 it adds optional [IFF](term:iff) to avoid firing on friendly aircraft, automatic lead and super-elevation that make shooting simpler and shorten the minimum range, and a slightly larger, lower-drag missile with a longer reach. Its delayed-impact fuze and a terminal manoeuvre aim the hit at the fuselage rather than the jet nozzle, and an extra charge sets off any rocket fuel left on impact. It resists [flares](term:decoy-flare) and infrared jammers better than the Strela-3. [[1]](#source-1) A tripod-mounted nose spike cuts drag. [[1]](#source-1) The 1998 Army handout lists passive IR/UV guidance with a cooled seeker and all-aspect capability. [[2]](#source-2)

The manufacturer gives a kill probability of 0.30 to 0.48 against an unprotected target, falling to 0.24 against flares and jamming. [[1]](#source-1)

## Envelope

The Igla-1 has a maximum range of 5,000 m (2.7 NM) and reaches targets up to 2,500 m (8,200 ft). [[1]](#source-1) The 1998 Army handout gives a range of 6 km+ and a 0 m minimum engagement altitude. [[2]](#source-2) Neither source gives a minimum range, so Figure 1 draws 5 km and 2,500 m with the inner edge and floor dashed.

## On the radar warning receiver

The SA-16 has no radar, so a radar warning receiver gives no warning of it.

## History

On 15 February 1991, during Desert Storm, an Iraqi SA-16 damaged the tail of a US Air Force [A-10A](record:a-10). An Igla-1E shot down a US [F-16](record:f-16) on 27 February 1991, and the pilot was captured; an RAF Tornado lost on 17 January may also have been an Igla-1E or a Strela-3. [[1]](#source-1) Over Kosovo, Igla-1E missiles hit two A-10s on 2 and 3 May 1999: one made an emergency landing at Skopje, and the warhead that hit the other failed to detonate. [[1]](#source-1) Igla-1E missiles were used in the 1994 shoot-down of the aircraft carrying the presidents of Rwanda and Burundi. [[1]](#source-1)
