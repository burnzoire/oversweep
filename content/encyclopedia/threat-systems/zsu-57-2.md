## Overview

The ZSU-57-2 mounts two 57 mm S-68 guns, a twin version of the towed [S-60](record:s-60), in a large open-topped turret on a lightened T-54 tank chassis. [[1]](#source-1) It officially entered Soviet service in 1955 and the first vehicles reached tank regiments in 1957. [[1]](#source-1) It had the heaviest firepower of any production self-propelled anti-aircraft gun of its time, but no radar, and from the mid-1960s the [ZSU-23-4](record:zsu-23-4) replaced it. [[1]](#source-1)

## How it engages

A sight adjuster sets the target's speed and course, estimated by eye, and its range, by eye or rangefinder, into an automatic plotter-type sight. The sight can follow a target at up to 350 m/s and a slant range of up to 5,500 m. [[1]](#source-1) With no search or fire-control radar it can only engage targets its crew can see, night firing was impractical, and it cannot fire aimed shots on the move. [[1]](#source-1) The two guns fire up to 210–240 rounds a minute together, 100–140 in practice; with only 300 rounds aboard, that is two to three minutes of fire. [[1]](#source-1)

The 2.8 kg shells are heavy: by the source's statistics a single hit could destroy a contemporary jet. [[1]](#source-1) The fragmentation shells destroy themselves 12 to 16 seconds after firing so they do not fall back to earth. [[1]](#source-1)

## Envelope

The guns reach 12 km horizontally and 8.8 km vertically, but the maximum effective vertical range is 4.5 km. [[1]](#source-1) Figure 1 combines that with the sight's 5.5 km slant range. The source judges a battery of four less effective than a battery of six radar-directed towed S-60s. [[1]](#source-1)

## History

North Vietnam used ZSU-57-2s from the 1972 Easter Offensive, and Egypt and Syria in 1967 and 1973, where the source says they were not very successful. [[1]](#source-1) Both sides used them in the Iran–Iraq War, where Iraqi vehicles could take target data from the radars of ZSU-23-4s or [SA-9](record:sa-9) and [SA-13](record:sa-13) vehicles. [[1]](#source-1) Egypt later fitted its remaining vehicles with radars, and Syria used at least one in its civil war in 2014. [[1]](#source-1)
