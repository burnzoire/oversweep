## A tank-killer from China Lake

The Rockeye is the Navy's anti-armour cluster bomb, and it reached the fleet in 1968. [[4]](#source-4) Navy ordnance training describes it plainly: an air-launched, free-fall weapon used against armoured vehicles, weighing 490 lb and holding 247 Mk 118 antitank bombs. [[1]](#source-1) It arrives in the fleet as a complete round, with fuze, lugs and arming wires already fitted. [[1]](#source-1)

## How it opens

The heart of the weapon is the Mk 7 dispenser, a cargo section with a nose fairing for the fuze and four spring-loaded folding fins at the tail. Two linear-shaped charges run lengthwise inside its walls. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) When the bomb is released, arming wires pull free, the fins snap open and the Mk 339 fuze starts timing. It fires 1.2 seconds later, or after 4.0 seconds if the pilot has selected the option time, and sets off the shaped charges, which cut the dispenser in half from nose to tail so that the bomblets spread out as they fall. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3)

Each Mk 118 bomblet is a small finned shaped charge. When its arming vane has spun up after it leaves the dispenser, it fires the moment it hits something hard such as armour or concrete; on soft ground it digs in until it slows enough for an inertia firing pin to set it off. [[3]](#source-3) A bomblet weighs 1.32 lb with 0.4 lb of explosive and can penetrate about 7.5 in of armour. [[4]](#source-4)

## Desert Storm

Rockeye saw its biggest use in the 1991 Gulf War. Of the 27,987 dropped, the Marine Corps dropped 15,828, against armour, artillery and troops, while the Navy dropped 6,813 and the Air Force 5,346. [[4]](#source-4) The design also lived on in the CBU-99 and CBU-100, which put the same 247 bomblets into new, thermally protected dispensers. [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4)
