## Keep the cross hairs on the target

Earlier anti-tank missiles, such as the SS.10 and ENTAC, were flown with a small joystick, and operators tended to overcorrect and swing the missile back and forth across the target. [[1]](#source-1) The TOW asked less of the gunner: keep the sight on the target, and the electronics, tracking an infrared lamp on the tail of the missile, work out the steering commands and send them down the wires. That is the concept now called SACLOS. [[1]](#source-1) It is one of the most widely used anti-tank missiles in the world. [[1]](#source-1)

## From the helicopter

The tube launch made the TOW hard to fire from a helicopter, because the missile had to be gathered into the tracker's view quickly after leaving the tube, and the requirement was dropped at first. [[1]](#source-1) A stabilised sight solved it: the XM26 system was developed for the UH-1, and the M65 became the main system on the AH-1 Cobra series. [[1]](#source-1) The British Army also fitted TOW to its Lynx helicopters, four missiles on each of two pylons. [[1]](#source-1)

## First blood

On 24 April 1972 the US 1st Combat Aerial TOW Team arrived in South Vietnam with two UH-1B helicopters, each carrying the XM26 system, to test the missile in combat. [[1]](#source-1) On 2 May, [UH-1s](record:uh-1h) firing TOWs destroyed North Vietnamese tanks near An Loc, the first time a US unit had destroyed enemy armour with American-designed guided missiles. [[1]](#source-1) For helicopters, the [laser-guided](term:lgw) [AGM-114 Hellfire](record:agm-114) began to replace the TOW in the 1980s. [[1]](#source-1)
