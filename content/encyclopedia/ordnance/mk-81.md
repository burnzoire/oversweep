## The smallest of the family

The Mark 80 series is the family of streamlined bombs that has armed American strike aircraft since Vietnam, and the Mk 81 is its smallest member: a 250 lb class bomb that actually weighs about 262 lb, nicknamed "Firecracker". [[1]](#source-1) It was developed in the 1950s, when the services wanted bombs that produced less drag when carried under fast jets, and like its larger siblings it is a thin steel case of which a large share of the weight is explosive. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3) Navy ordnance training describes the Mk 80 bombs as low-drag general-purpose bombs, about 45 per cent explosive by weight, used in most bombing operations. [[4]](#source-4)

## Snake and nape

The Mk 81 earned its place in the Vietnam War's close air support. Fitted with the Mk 14 tail retarding device, which springs open after release to slow the bomb, it became the Mk 81 Snakeye. The extra time of fall, and the forgiving drop envelope that came with it, allowed bombing runs at very low level and slower speed. [[1]](#source-1) A typical support load of the period was known as "snake and nape": 250 lb Snakeyes and napalm canisters. [[1]](#source-1)

## Too small for most jobs

US forces judged the Mk 81's blast too small for most of their targets, and it was soon dropped from wide use, although licence-built copies remain in service in other countries. [[1]](#source-1) It never disappeared entirely: the Air Force's 2021 ammunition budget still describes the Mk-81 as a 250 lb class bomb "designed for soft, fragment sensitive targets, such as troops, POL, and radars". [[2]](#source-2) A guided version, the GBU-29 JDAM, was started because a small bomb causes less collateral damage, but it was cancelled in favour of the purpose-built Small Diameter Bomb. [[1]](#source-1)
