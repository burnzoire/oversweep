## The heat-seeker everyone copied

The Sidewinder came out of the Navy's weapons laboratory at China Lake in the Mojave Desert, and it was built to be small, light and cheap. It entered Navy service in 1956 and Air Force service in 1964, and more than 110,000 have been made for the United States and 27 other nations, which makes it the most widely used air-to-air missile in the West. [[1]](#source-1) The Navy's ordnance manual describes the AIM-9 series as supersonic missiles with passive infrared target detection, proportional navigation guidance and torque-balance control, carried by the [F-14](record:f-14) and the [F/A-18](record:fa-18c). [[2]](#source-2)

Passive is the important word: the missile sends nothing out. Its seeker [homes](term:homing) on heat, and the early versions could only see the hot exhaust of a jet engine, so they had to be fired from behind the target. Later seekers were cooled with liquid nitrogen from the launcher, which let them track the heat that air friction builds on a fast aircraft from any angle. [[1]](#source-1) In brevity a Sidewinder shot is "[Fox](term:fox) Two", the call for an infrared-guided missile.

## Inside the missile

The guidance and control section holds the infrared seeker, an electronics assembly that turns what the seeker sees into steering commands, and a gas servo that moves the four control fins. Behind it the target detector, a narrow-beam active-optical [proximity fuze](term:proximity-fuze), senses an aircraft within the warhead's burst range and sends the firing signal. The Mk 36 rocket motor drives it, and four wings at the tail each carry a rolleron to steady the missile in flight. [[3]](#source-3)

## From Taiwan to today

The first Sidewinders fired in combat were launched by Taiwanese [F-86 Sabres](record:f-86) over the Taiwan Strait on 24 September 1958, after a small US Marine Corps team modified the aircraft to carry them. [[1]](#source-1) Over Vietnam the AIM-9E hit only about 8% of the time, which drove the all-aspect AIM-9L; that version proved itself in the 1982 Falklands War and over Lebanon the same year. [[1]](#source-1) The latest AIM-9X, with an imaging infrared seeker, remains standard equipment in most Western-aligned air forces. [[1]](#source-1)
