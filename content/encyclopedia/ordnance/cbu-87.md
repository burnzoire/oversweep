## Three effects in one bomblet

The CBU-87 was introduced in 1986 to replace the cluster bombs the US Air Force had used in Vietnam. [[1]](#source-1) Its name, Combined Effects Munition, describes what is inside: 202 BLU-97/B bomblets, yellow cylinders 20 cm long and 6 cm across, each combining three ways of doing damage. A shaped charge in the nose can pierce armour, the scored steel case bursts into fragments against people and soft vehicles, and a zirconium ring starts fires. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)

## Choosing the pattern

The CBU-87 is a free-fall weapon: once released it receives no further instructions, and it was designed to be dropped at any altitude and any airspeed. [[1]](#source-1) After release the canister begins to spin, at one of six selectable rates, and at a set height it opens and releases the bomblets. Each bomblet deploys a small inflatable decelerator that slows and orients it. The spin rate and the opening height together decide the size of the pattern on the ground, from about 20 × 20 m for a low opening and slow spin to about 120 × 240 m for a high opening and fast spin. [[1]](#source-1)

## Desert Storm and its aftermath

The Air Force dropped 10,035 CBU-87s in Desert Storm, and about 1,100 cluster bombs, mostly CBU-87s, in the 1999 air campaign over Kosovo. [[1]](#source-1) The weapon's weakness is what it leaves behind. Manufacturers claimed a failure rate of about 5 per cent, roughly ten duds per bomb; campaigners measured more after Kosovo. [[1]](#source-1) After the Gulf War some 100,000 unexploded bomblets were estimated to lie in Kuwait and Iraq, so sensitive that the Army advised against moving them, and clearing them cost lives, including seven US combat engineers killed clearing the runway at As Salam air base. [[2]](#source-2)

A guidance tail turned the CBU-87 into the CBU-103, which is much more accurate. [[1]](#source-1)
