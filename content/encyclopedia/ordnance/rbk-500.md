## A case for small bombs

RBK stands for a disposable bomb cluster: a thin-walled bomb case built to carry small bombs of up to 20 kg and scatter them over a target. [[3]](#source-3) The name adds the calibre and then the load, so an RBK-500 AO-2.5RTM is a 500 kg class case full of 2.5 kg fragmentation bomblets. [[3]](#source-3) Some RBKs have a removable nose fairing, so that the same bomb can hang on an external pylon or fit inside a weapons bay. [[3]](#source-3)

Soviet designers used two ways of getting the bomblets out. In one, a remote [fuze](term:fuze) fires an expelling charge that drives a disc and a central tube backwards through the case, pushing the bomblets out of the tail. In the other, a charge in a perforated central tube blows open a weakened seam along the side, scattering the load over a wider area. [[3]](#source-3)

## One case, many loads

What the RBK-500 carries depends on the target. The same 450 mm case can hold 108 or 126 fragmentation bomblets for troops and soft vehicles, 268 or 352 small shaped-charge bombs against armour, a dozen concrete-breaking bombs, or 297 incendiaries; loaded weights run from about 427 to 525 kg. [[3]](#source-3) The smaller RBK-250 is a 250 kg class bomb; its incendiary ZAB-2.5M version carries 48 bomblets and weighs 195 kg. [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3)

The most advanced load is the SPBE-D: 15 Motiv-3 anti-tank submunitions developed by NPO Bazalt, each with a dual-mode infrared seeker. This version entered Soviet Air Force service in 1987. [[1]](#source-1)

## In Ukraine

In November 2023, during the Russian invasion of Ukraine, Russian forces were reported to be dropping RBK-500s fitted with UMPK glide and correction kits in Donetsk Oblast, the first time the combination had been seen in public video. [[1]](#source-1)
