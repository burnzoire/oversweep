## A kit, not a new missile

The APKWS (Advanced Precision Kill Weapon System) is not a new missile but a kit. A guidance section the size of a soda can is screwed in between the motor and the warhead of an ordinary [Hydra 70](record:hydra-70) 2.75 in rocket, turning it into a [laser-guided](term:lgw) [precision weapon](term:pgm). [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) Ordnance crews remove the warhead, fit the guidance unit to the motor, put the warhead back on, set the laser code and switch the unit on. [[2]](#source-2) The rocket uses the existing launchers, motors, warheads and [fuzes](term:fuze). [[1]](#source-1)

It fills the gap between unguided rockets and the [Hellfire](record:agm-114): accurate enough for a single vehicle or a small team, with a smaller warhead that limits collateral damage, at about a third of the cost and weight of other laser-guided weapons. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) A helicopter that carries pods of rockets can now carry a pod of precision shots; the program office quoted "up to 76" precision kills on one assault-support sortie. [[2]](#source-2)

## Seekers in the wings

Most laser-guided weapons look out through the nose. The APKWS cannot, because the Hydra warhead sits there, so its WGU-59/B mid-body unit puts a laser seeker in the leading edge of each of its four canards. [[1]](#source-1) The seeker optics deploy about half a second after launch. [[1]](#source-1) Like any semi-active laser weapon, it needs a designator, on the launching aircraft, another aircraft or the ground, lasing the target on the code set in the rocket. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The pilot fires it much like an unguided rocket. [[2]](#source-2) At its minimum range of about 1,100 m from a helicopter, it reaches the target in under five seconds. [[1]](#source-1)

## From helicopters to fighters, and against drones

The US Army began the program in 1996, and the Navy took it over in 2008 after the first version was cancelled. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The Marine Corps fielded the APKWS II on AH-1W and UH-1Y helicopters in Afghanistan in March 2012. [[2]](#source-2) Fixed-wing versions followed: the AV-8B Harrier in 2016, the A-10 and F-16 the same year and Marine F/A-18 Hornets in 2018. [[1]](#source-1) From 2019 the US Air Force tested it from fighters against drones and low-flying cruise missiles, cueing it with a targeting pod, a far cheaper shot than an [AIM-120](record:aim-120). [[1]](#source-1)
