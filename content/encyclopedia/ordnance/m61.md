## Volume of fire

At the end of the Second World War the US Army Air Forces saw that jet speeds would make it very hard to score enough hits with existing guns, unless the rate of fire went up a great deal. General Electric's answer, designed from 1946, was an old idea driven by modern power: Richard Gatling's revolving cluster of barrels. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The Navy's ordnance manual describes the M61A1 as a six-barrel, rotary-action, hydraulically driven and electrically controlled gun. Each barrel fires once per revolution, and the gun can fire 4,000 to 7,200 rounds per minute; in Navy aircraft the pilot chooses 4,000 or 6,000. [[2]](#source-2)

Early tests in the F-104 Starfighter showed that linked ammunition misfed and that the discarded links were a foreign-object hazard, so the M61A1 got a linkless feed. It became the standard gun of US fighters, and the M61 family has been their main cannon since 1959. [[1]](#source-1) The conveyor that feeds the gun also carries spent cases and unfired rounds back to the drum. [[2]](#source-2)

## In the Hornet

In the [F/A-18](record:fa-18c) the gun sits on the centreline in the nose, firing through a blast diffuser just ahead of the windscreen. The pilot can use it in two air-to-ground modes, [CCIP](term:ccip) and manual, and in three air-to-air modes, called director, disturbed and cage. [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4) The drum holds up to 578 rounds. For training, ground crews can set a round limiter so that one load lasts two or three gunnery sorties, and the pilot can choose bursts of a preset length or continuous fire. [[4]](#source-4)
