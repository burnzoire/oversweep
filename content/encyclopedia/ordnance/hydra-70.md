## A bomber-killer that missed

The 2.75 in rocket began as an answer to a Cold War problem. Jet bombers closed so fast that a fighter pilot had only a moment to fire, and wartime experience suggested that machine guns could not reliably bring a bomber down. The Germans had shown that volleys of unguided rockets could. [[2]](#source-2) In the late 1940s the US Navy developed the Mk 4 Folding-Fin Aerial Rocket, about 4 ft long and 18.5 lb, whose fins flipped out as it left the tube. Crews called it "Mighty Mouse", after the cartoon character. [[2]](#source-2) In the early 1950s it was the main armament of interceptors such as the F-86D, F-89 and F-94C, carried in large numbers and fired in volleys. [[2]](#source-2)

As an air-to-air weapon it was a disappointment. One hit could destroy a bomber, but the rockets scattered so widely that a 24-round volley covered an area the size of a football field. In August 1956 two Air Force F-89s fired 208 rockets at a runaway Navy target drone and failed to bring it down. By the end of the decade guided missiles had replaced it. [[2]](#source-2)

## A second career

Against the ground, where a volley's spread mattered less, the rocket found its true role, above all on the new armed helicopters. Fitted with the more powerful Mk 40 motor and a range of warheads, it served in Korea and Vietnam from about 20 different aircraft, fixed-wing and rotary. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The Navy's ordnance manual still notes that rockets began in aircraft armament in the Second World War because they gave a heavy blow without recoil, and that the Navy's two rockets are the 2.75 in "Mighty Mouse" and the 5 in Zuni. [[6]](#source-6) It describes FFARs fired in large numbers to make a shotgun pattern, and a low-spin version for helicopters and low-speed aircraft which, because it spins, is fired one at a time rather than rippled. [[4]](#source-4)

## Hydra 70

The modern Hydra 70 is the same 2.75 in rocket with the Mk 66 motor. Its propellant gives considerably more thrust, its fins wrap around the body and flick sideways to open, and its slightly angled nozzles start it spinning while it is still in the tube. [[1]](#source-1) The motor burns for just over a second and the rocket leaves the tube at 148 ft/s, reaching 2,425 ft/s at burnout. [[1]](#source-1) The commonest warhead, the M151 "10-pounder", is high-explosive fragmentation, the kind the Navy manual describes for use against troops and light targets such as trucks and parked aircraft. [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3)

Today it is carried by every US service. The Army fires it from Apaches in 19-tube M261 launchers and from Kiowa Warriors in seven-tube M260s; the Marine Corps carries it on the AH-1W and AH-1Z and on the UH-1; and the Navy and Air Force use it too. [[1]](#source-1) The Navy's launchers are the seven-tube LAU-68 and the 19-tube LAU-61, both reusable and able to fire singly or in ripple. [[5]](#source-5)
