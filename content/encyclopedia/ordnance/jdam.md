## A kit, not a bomb

The JDAM is not a weapon of its own but a bolt-on guidance package. A tail section with control surfaces, a strake kit on the bomb body and a combined inertial and GPS guidance unit turn an unguided bomb into an all-weather precision weapon, and the bomb then takes a GBU designation in place of its Mk 80 or BLU name. [[1]](#source-1) The Air Force and Navy developed it together, hence "joint". [[1]](#source-1) The 2,000 lb version is the GBU-31, the 1,000 lb the GBU-32 [[2]](#source-2), and the 500 lb the GBU-38. [[1]](#source-1)

## Why GPS

Desert Storm showed the gap it filled. [Laser-guided bombs](term:lgw) were very accurate in clear air, but dust, smoke, fog or cloud could break their lock on the laser spot, and they could not be released from high altitude. [[1]](#source-1) A JDAM needs no spot. The mission plan, with the release envelope, target coordinates and terminal parameters, is loaded before take-off; the weapon aligns its inertial system with the aircraft's while carried, and once released inside the launch acceptable region it guides itself whatever the weather. [[2]](#source-2) If GPS is jammed after release, the inertial system carries on alone with a wider error, 30 m CEP against 13 m. [[2]](#source-2) It can be dropped from very low to very high altitude, in level flight, a dive, a toss or a [loft](term:loft), and several can be aimed at different targets in a single pass. [[2]](#source-2)

## In combat

JDAM and the B-2 made their combat debuts together over Kosovo in 1999, when B-2s flying 30-hour round trips from Missouri delivered more than 650 of them. [[1]](#source-1) The Laser JDAM came out of Afghanistan and Iraq: it adds a laser seeker to the nose so the bomb can hit moving targets, while keeping its GPS accuracy if no laser is available. The GBU-54 made its combat debut in August 2008, when an [F-16](record:f-16) of the 77th Fighter Squadron hit a moving vehicle in Iraq. [[1]](#source-1) By January 2024 some 550,000 JDAM kits had been built. [[1]](#source-1)
