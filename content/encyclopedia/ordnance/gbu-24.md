## Built for low level

The GBU-24 is a 2,000 lb [Mk 84](record:mk-84) or BLU-109 penetrator with a Paveway III low-level laser-guided bomb kit. The kit was developed in response to sophisticated air defences, poor visibility and low ceilings, which push an attacker down low. [[2]](#source-2) It swaps the Paveway II's bang-bang steering for proportional guidance, adds a more sensitive seeker with a larger field of regard, and has bigger wings for manoeuvrability. [[2]](#source-2) The result glides farther than a Paveway II, but costs more, so it suits well-defended, high-value targets. [[1]](#source-1)

## Why the guidance matters

The FAS explains the problem it solves. One way to deliver a laser-guided bomb from low altitude is to [loft](term:loft) it, but if an older bomb sees the laser spot too soon after release it pulls down below the trajectory it needs and lands well short. The GBU-24 is far less sensitive to how it was released and is not affected by early designation: after a low-altitude release it holds level flight while it looks for the spot, and if it never finds it, it flies on past the target to land long rather than short of friendly positions. [[2]](#source-2) Its wide field of view also allows a "point shoot" delivery, in which the pilot points the aircraft at the target and releases without an accurate dive or tracking run. [[2]](#source-2)

## In service

The pulse-coded spot can come from the launch aircraft, from another aircraft ("buddy lasing") or from a ground designator; if the laser is lost, the bomb stops guiding and falls on a roughly ballistic path. [[1]](#source-1) In the Gulf War all 1,181 GBU-24s were released by F-111Fs. [[2]](#source-2) Navy [F-14s](record:f-14) dropped twenty-three during the 2003 invasion of Iraq. [[1]](#source-1)
