## A German idea

The S-5 began as a study of a captured weapon. In 1946 the Soviet Nudelman design bureau, then OKB-16, began research on unguided air-to-air rockets, and among the things it examined closely was the German 55 mm R4M "Orkan" air-to-air rocket of the Second World War. [[1]](#source-1) The project was only formally funded in 1951, as an air-to-air weapon for the [MiG-19](record:mig-19), and tests on [MiG-15s](record:mig-15) and MiG-17s ran until January 1955. They showed that the rocket did not perform as hoped against aircraft, but it was accepted into service anyway in April 1955 as the ARS-57, designated S-5. [[1]](#source-1)

## Small and many

In service the S-5 has been used against ground area targets. It is a small steel rocket with a solid motor that burns for 1.1 seconds, covering about 300 m, and eight fins that fold around the body in the tube and spring open as it leaves, spinning it at about 750 rpm. [[1]](#source-1) It is only 55 mm across but is fired from 57 mm tubes, which is why its pods carry the number 57. [[1]](#source-1)

The S-5 is used in numbers. The first ORO-57 launchers held four, eight or 16 rockets, with the eight-round ORO-57K typical on the [MiG-19](record:mig-19). From the early 1960s the 16-round UB-16-57 became standard on both aircraft and helicopters, its name meaning "universal block" because it fitted ordinary bomb racks, and in the 1970s the 32-round UB-32 followed for heavier aircraft. [[1]](#source-1)

## Revived

Production ended in 1990, and by 2013 only Belarus and Bulgaria still made the S-5. In 2019 Russia announced it would build it again as the S-5U: longer, heavier, with wrap-around fins and a universal 0.8 kg warhead that combines armour penetration, fragments and incendiary elements, while still fitting the old pods. [[1]](#source-1)
