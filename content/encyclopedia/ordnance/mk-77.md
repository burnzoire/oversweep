## Napalm's successor

The Mk 77 is a canister of thickened fuel: the United States military's main incendiary weapon and the direct successor to napalm. [[1]](#source-1) In the Second World War the American napalm bomb was the Mark 47, and in Vietnam a typical close air support load paired retarded bombs with napalm canisters: "snake and nape". [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) The Mk 77 replaced napalm's gasoline, polystyrene and benzene mix with a kerosene-based fuel lower in benzene. Its effects on the target are similar. [[1]](#source-1)

## Lighter over time

The first version, the Mod 0, weighed 750 lb and carried 110 US gallons of fuel. Later versions carried 75 gallons and weighed roughly 500 to 550 lb. The Mod 4 dropped in the 1991 Gulf War and the Mod 5 used in 2003 each weighed about 507 lb, the Mod 5 filled with JP-4, JP-5 or JP-8 jet fuel and a thickener. [[1]](#source-1) Two bigger fire bombs, the Mk 78 and Mk 79, have left service. [[1]](#source-1)

## A Marine Corps weapon

In recent wars the Mk 77 has mainly been a Marine Corps weapon. Marines used it in Desert Storm and again in the 2003 invasion of Iraq; about 500 were dropped, reportedly mostly on Iraqi trenches filled with oil. It was also used at Tora Bora in Afghanistan. [[1]](#source-1) The I Marine Expeditionary Force reported using 30 between 31 March and 2 April 2003, against military targets away from civilian areas, and the Pentagon told the British government that because of its limited accuracy the Mk 77 is not generally used in towns or where civilians gather. [[1]](#source-1)

Incendiary weapons carry legal limits. Protocol III of the 1980 Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons bans air-delivered incendiaries against civilians, and against military targets inside concentrations of civilians. The United States reserved the right to use them against such targets where doing so would cause fewer casualties or less collateral damage than other weapons. [[1]](#source-1)
