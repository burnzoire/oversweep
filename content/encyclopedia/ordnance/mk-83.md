## The Navy's thousand-pounder

The Mk 83 sits in the middle of the Mark 80 family: a 1,000 lb class bomb whose real weight runs from 985 to 1,030 lb with its fuzes and fins, built around 445 lb of tritonal. [[1]](#source-1) It is the bomb most associated with the US Navy, which uses it more than any other service, and the photographs of it tend to be taken on carrier decks. [[1]](#source-1) Like the rest of the series it has the long, slim shape designed to cut drag on fast aircraft, and it did not reach combat until Vietnam. [[5]](#source-5)

## Slowing a heavy bomb

A low-level attack with a heavy bomb raises the same problem as with a light one, only worse: an unretarded bomb arrives at the target with the aircraft that dropped it. The Navy's answer for the Mk 83 was a pair of retarding tails. The MAU-91A/B is a four-plate fin that can be dropped closed, as a low-drag bomb, or opened, so the plates catch the air and slow the bomb. [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4) The BSU-85/B goes further: it is an air-inflatable retarder for delivery at very low altitude. [[4]](#source-4) Either way, the aim is the one the manual gives for all retarded delivery: to give the aircraft time to get clear before the bomb arrives. [[4]](#source-4)

## A warhead and a mine

Today the Mk 83 is as likely to be the business end of a guided weapon as a free-fall bomb. Filled with insensitive PBXN-109 explosive it becomes the BLU-110, the warhead of the GBU-16 Paveway II laser-guided bomb and the GBU-32 JDAM, and the Air Force carries it on the F-22A in JDAM form. [[1]](#source-1) It also goes to sea in another guise: with the Mk 75 modification kit, the Mk 83 becomes the Mk 40 Destructor, an air-laid mine. [[6]](#source-6) Abroad, Pakistan builds guided and range-extended bombs around it; in 2019 two Pakistan Air Force JF-17s used Mk 83 range-extension-kit bombs against targets in Indian-administered Kashmir.
