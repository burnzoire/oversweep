## A Mk 84 that steers

The GBU-10 is a 2,000 lb [Mk 84](record:mk-84) general-purpose bomb, or a BLU-109 penetrator, with a Paveway II laser guidance kit added: a seeker on the nose and wings for guidance. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) Someone lights the target with a laser designator and the bomb guides to the spot of laser energy reflected from it. [[2]](#source-2) The FAS lists a range of 8 NM and a circular error probable of 9 m. [[2]](#source-2)

## Paveway I and II

Paveway kits are built by Texas Instruments' successors, Raytheon, and by Lockheed Martin. The early, fixed-wing Paveway I kit gave way in the early 1970s to the Paveway II, with a simpler, more reliable seeker and pop-out rear wings. [[3]](#source-3) The FAS lists what the Paveway II improved: lighter injection-moulded seeker optics, a more sensitive detector with a field of view 30 per cent wider, a shorter battery delay after release, more canard deflection, laser coding and folding wings. [[2]](#source-2) Both generations steer with "bang-bang" control, deflecting the canards fully rather than in proportion, which makes the bomb wobble along its path and bleeds energy, limiting range. [[3]](#source-3)

## In combat

The Air Force says the GBU-10 hit 78 per cent of its targets in Desert Storm. Of the 2,637 dropped, over a third came from F-111Fs and the rest from F-117s, [F-15Es](record:f-15e) and Navy and Marine Corps aircraft, mainly against bridges, Scud launchers, command nodes and bunkers. [[2]](#source-2) On 14 February 1991 an F-15E crew even scored an air-to-air kill with one, destroying an Iraqi Mi-24 helicopter. [[1]](#source-1) Later Enhanced Paveway versions added GPS/INS guidance; the Royal Air Force has used Raytheon's over Afghanistan and Iraq. [[1]](#source-1)
