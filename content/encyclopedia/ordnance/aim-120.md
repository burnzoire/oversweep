## Fire and forget

The AMRAAM's advantage over the [Sparrow](record:aim-7) is that it carries its own radar. It flies most of the way on an inertial reference unit, takes updates from the launching aircraft over a data link if they are sent, and switches on its own active radar seeker as it closes on the target. Wikipedia calls this fire-and-forget: the crew no longer has to guide the missile, so it can aim and fire several at different targets at once. [[1]](#source-1) Firing an active radar missile is called "[Fox](term:fox) Three".

The Navy's ordnance manual lists four sections. The guidance section holds the radome, seeker, inertial reference unit and target detection device. The armament section holds the WDU-33/B fragmenting warhead and the FZU-49/B safe and arm [fuze](term:fuze), and the propulsion section a dual-thrust, low-smoke solid [rocket](term:rocket) motor. The control section has four electromechanical actuators and the data link. [[2]](#source-2)

## In combat

The AMRAAM's first combat kill came on 27 December 1992, when a US Air Force [F-16D](record:f-16) shot down an Iraqi MiG-25 over the southern no-fly zone; a second followed in January 1993. [[1]](#source-1) It has since largely replaced the Sparrow as the main US beyond-visual-range missile, and more than 14,000 have been built for the US Air Force, the Navy and 33 other customers. [[1]](#source-1)
