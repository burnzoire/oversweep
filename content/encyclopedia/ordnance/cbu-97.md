## A cluster bomb that looks for tanks

Most cluster bombs scatter their bomblets and let them fall where they will. The CBU-97 does something cleverer. Inside its dispenser are ten BLU-108 submunitions, and inside each of those are four hockey-puck-shaped projectiles called Skeets, forty in all. Each Skeet carries sensors and an explosively formed penetrator: a charge that fires a slug downward fast enough to go through armour. [[1]](#source-1)

## How it opens

As the bomb approaches its aim point, a cutting charge splits the dispenser skin into three panels, which the airflow peels away. An airbag pushes out the submunitions, five from the front bay and then five from the rear. Each one lowers on a parachute, the ten spreading roughly 100 ft apart, then drops the parachute, fires a small rocket that stops its fall and spins it, and throws out its Skeets in pairs. [[1]](#source-1) Each spinning Skeet wobbles in a cone, sweeping a circle of ground. Its laser measures the height of what lies below, looking for the outline of a vehicle; its infrared sensor looks for the heat of an engine. When both match, it fires downward into the target's roof, where even a main battle tank is thinly armoured. [[1]](#source-1) Together the forty Skeets search an area of about 1,500 by 500 ft. [[1]](#source-1)

## Fewer duds

The design deals directly with the problem that dogs older cluster bombs. A Skeet that finds nothing destroys itself about 50 ft above the ground, and a back-up timer disables it if that fails, giving an unexploded-ordnance rate below 1 per cent. [[1]](#source-1)

## Service

The weapon was designed in the Cold War for fighter-bombers attacking Soviet armour at low level, below radar cover, but its release envelope runs from 200 ft to 20,000 ft above the ground. [[1]](#source-1) It has been in production since 1992; it was deployed but not used over Kosovo in 1999 and was first fired in combat during the 2003 invasion of Iraq. [[1]](#source-1) The United States last bought the weapon in 2007, India ordered 512 of the guided CBU-105 for its Jaguars in 2010, and Textron stopped production in 2016, citing low demand and international controversy. [[1]](#source-1)
