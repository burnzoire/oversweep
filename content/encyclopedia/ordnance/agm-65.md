## Fire and forget

The Maverick replaced the AGM-12 Bullpup, whose launch aircraft had to fly straight at the target while the pilot guided the missile, exposed to the defences the whole way. [[1]](#source-1) Hughes built the Maverick as a fire-and-forget missile, the first to use an electronic contrast seeker, and it entered Air Force service in August 1972. [[1]](#source-1) The FAS calls this "launch and leave": the pilot fires and can immediately manoeuvre or attack another target while the missile guides itself. [[2]](#source-2) It has become the most widely produced precision-guided missile in the Western world. [[1]](#source-1)

## Seekers and warheads

In the original TV-guided Maverick A, the seeker's picture appears on a cockpit screen; the pilot selects the target, centres the cross hairs on it and locks on before launch. [[2]](#source-2) Later versions use imaging infrared, for night and haze, or laser guidance. [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2) There are two warheads: a shaped charge fired by a contact fuze in the nose, and a heavyweight penetrator with a delayed fuze that drives into large, hard targets before it goes off. [[2]](#source-2) Mavericks can be launched from high altitude down to treetop level. [[2]](#source-2)

## In combat

The Maverick has fought in Vietnam, the Yom Kippur War, the Iran–Iraq War and the Gulf War. [[1]](#source-1) In Desert Storm, [F-15Es](record:f-15e), [F/A-18s](record:fa-18c), [AV-8Bs](record:av-8b), [F-16s](record:f-16) and [A-10s](record:a-10), mainly the last two, fired more than 5,000 Mavericks [[1]](#source-1), and according to the Air Force it hit 85 per cent of its targets. [[2]](#source-2)
